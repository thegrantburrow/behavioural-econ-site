# Authentic Voice

## Why this exists

On 2026-08-22 the owner flagged a sentence from a freshly written experiment blueprint: "That's a proposed use, not a tested one." His words: "I've picked up you writing like AI again. This not that etc." He was right. That sentence does no real work its plainer version couldn't do: the "not a tested one" half exists purely to make the sentence sound considered. A site whose entire pitch is real examples and real research over sounding smart cannot afford to read like a template. This skill exists so that catch doesn't have to happen twice.

## The research behind this

Ten hallmarks, drawn from documented patterns in AI-generated text (Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup style guide, Forbes' recurring "giveaway signs" series, and independent writing-craft audits), cross-checked against this site's own content:

1. **The contrastive reveal ("X, not Y").** A claim immediately followed by its negated opposite, used for rhetorical weight rather than because the negation adds information. This is the one that got caught. See below for the full test.
2. **Rule-of-three padding.** Grouping ideas or examples in threes (or fours) as a default rhythm, regardless of whether the content actually has that many parts.
3. **Hollow abstractions.** "boundaries," "landscape," "tapestry," "testament to," "at its core," "delve into," "unlock," "leverage," "elevate," "seamless," "robust," "game-changer": words that sound considered but could sit in front of any noun without changing the meaning.
4. **Throat-clearing announcements.** "Here's the kicker," "But here's the thing," "It's worth noting that," "It's important to note that": a promise of a payoff, stated instead of delivered.
5. **Stacked formal transitions.** "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "Consequently" opening consecutive sentences or paragraphs like a debate outline.
6. **Redundant recap closings.** A final paragraph that opens "In conclusion," "In summary," "Ultimately," or "At the end of the day" and then just restates the paragraph before it, instead of ending on an actual last thought.
7. **Uniform sentence rhythm.** Every sentence roughly the same length, every paragraph the same shape. Smooth, but mechanical; real writing has short sentences next to long ones.
8. **Superficial analysis dressed as depth.** Confident, grammatical prose that doesn't actually add a new fact or a real insight, just restates the premise with more words.
9. **Stacked hedges.** "It's important to note," "it should be noted," "arguably," piled up to soften a claim instead of just making it.
10. **Personified abstractions.** "The data speaks for itself," "the research reveals," "this shift represents": treating a dataset or a trend as an agent with something to say.

Sources: [Forbes, "15 New Giveaway Signs of AI Writing" (Feb & May 2026)](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jodiecook/2026/02/03/the-15-new-giveaway-signs-of-ai-generated-content-in-february-2026/), [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), [Grammarly, "Common Words and Phrases in AI-Generated Content"](https://www.grammarly.com/blog/ai/common-ai-words/).

## The 2026-08-22 site audit: what a real pass looks like

Grepping the whole site for pattern #1 turned up 102 raw hits. Nearly all of them were fine. Going through every one taught the actual lesson this skill exists to encode: **a negation only earns its place when it's carrying real weight.**

Kept, because the distinction is the actual content:
- "Choices are made by relative comparison, not absolute judgment." (Decoy Effect's core mechanism, the sentence *is* the finding)
- "paraphrased from the abstract and secondary academic summaries, not quoted verbatim" (a citation-honesty disclosure required by this site's own sourcing standard, deleting it deletes real information)
- "Internal validity: A real grocery store, real purchases, not stated preference" (revealed vs. stated preference is a load-bearing methodological distinction in behavioural economics, not decoration)
- Bolded pull-quote lines like "Only the zero moved." and "The set, not the amount, became the target." (this site's deliberate, consistent `.flow-insight` / illustration-caption convention, a punchy standalone headline, not a mid-paragraph crutch)

Fixed, because the negation was empty rhetorical weight with no real payoff:
- "That's a proposed use, not a tested one" &rarr; "The principle proposes this; nobody has tested it." (two plain sentences, same information, no reveal)
- "...is a legitimate design choice, but it's not a neutral one" &rarr; "...is a legitimate design choice. It also shapes how much people spend." (says what it actually shapes, instead of just negating "neutral")
- "The first half of this is a strategy problem, not a statistics one" &rarr; "The first half of this is a strategy problem. Better statistics can't fix it." (states the real consequence instead of naming what it isn't)
- "That isn't friction added to punish a customer, it's putting back the cues a card removed" &rarr; "It puts back the cues a card removed." (the full "It's not X, it's Y" sentence shape, cut straight to the actual claim)
- Two uses of "perceived proximity, not real distance" inside one experiment's Theory and Hypothesis blocks &rarr; varied the second one entirely, because repeating the identical construction twice in one piece is itself the tic, even when each instance is individually defensible

## The test, before calling any sentence finished

For every "X, not Y" (or "It's not X, it's Y," or "isn't a Y, it's a Z") construction:

1. **Delete the negated half. Does the sentence lose real information, or just lose punch?** If a reader would learn something true and specific from "not Y" that they couldn't get from "X" alone, keep it. If "not Y" is just there to make "X" sound more considered, cut it or replace the whole sentence with a plainer claim.
2. **Is this a standalone bolded headline (a `.flow-insight`, a `.lesson`, an illustration caption), or is it sitting mid-paragraph as connective tissue?** This site's punchy one-line pull-quotes are a deliberate, consistent design convention, not the tic. The tic shows up embedded in ordinary explanatory prose, dressing up a plain sentence as a considered one.
3. **Has this exact construction already appeared once in this same piece?** One real contrastive clarification is a clarification. Two in one article is a crutch. Say the second one a different way, or don't say it at all.
4. **Would a specific person actually say this out loud, in this shape, making this particular point?** "A specific, citable, replicated finding, not a vibe" survives this test in a first-person field-session narrative because it has real personality and a real referent. "That's a proposed use, not a tested one" doesn't survive it anywhere; nobody talks like that.
5. **Was "Y" already stated nearby, so this "X, not Y" is just restating it in contrastive shape?** A negation can pass question 1 (it's individually true and specific) and still be the tic, if the reader already learned Y from an immediately preceding heading, list item, or sentence. Check the surrounding structure, not just the sentence in isolation, for whether Y was already on the page. See the 2026-08-24 catch below: this is the question that would have caught it, and none of the first four would have.

Run the same five-question test against hallmarks 2 through 10 above when reviewing a full piece, not just #1. A rule-of-three that's actually three things is fine; a rule-of-three imposed on two things and a stretch isn't. A transition word is fine once; three in a row reads like an outline.

## The 2026-08-23 catch: CV and evidence-table content has its own tells

This skill was written against flowing editorial prose (principle articles, field sessions). Applying it to a bulleted CV-style evidence table for Grant's UTS Recognition of Prior Learning application, on his direct instruction to "ensure written as me not AI," surfaced two real failure modes the original ten don't name directly, both confirmed by grep against the actual file, not guessed at:

11. **Verbatim phrase reuse across entries.** The same descriptive phrase copy-pasted into two different bullet points ("anchoring, salience, default effects" used identically for two different employers; "structurally different" used twice to describe two different things). In flowing prose this reads as repetition; in a table where a reader compares rows side by side, it reads as templated filler dressed up as two separate observations. Fix: say the same underlying fact in a different way each time, or cut the repeat and let the first instance stand.
12. **Mechanical uniform bullet architecture.** Every bullet following the identical shape (`<b>Title, Company</b> (dates): verb-led clause`) is the bulleted-list version of hallmark #7's uniform sentence rhythm, and just as mechanical: a reader's eye slides over it as a template rather than reading each line as its own claim. Fix: vary which part of the bullet leads. Some open with the role, some with the outcome, some with the fact itself; the dates and title can trail in a clause instead of always fronting the sentence.

Hallmark #8 (superficial analysis dressed as depth) also has a sharper version in this context: **confidently describing what a role "must have involved" when the only real source is a bare job title and date range.** A CV bullet inferring specific responsibilities from a title alone, with no supporting detail in the actual source document, reads as embellishment even when every individual word is plausible. Fix: state the verifiable fact (title, dates, tenure) plainly, and only add interpretive claims about what the role actually entailed when the source material (a description, a stated achievement, a number) actually supports it.

## The 2026-08-24 catch: the audit never checked headings, and "true" isn't the same as "not already said"

The owner flagged a live heading on `nobody-owns-a-bias.html`: "2002: the Nobel goes to economics, not psychology." His words: "This is a hallmark of ai writing... Root cause. Fix and back fix and moving forward."

**Root cause, part one: the 2026-08-22 audit only ever greped body prose.** That heading had sat on the page since before this skill existed, and the 102-hit grep that produced the "kept vs. fixed" list above never surfaced it, because nobody thought to point the same grep at `<h1>` through `<h6>` text. A fresh heading-only grep across the site turned up a dozen more never-reviewed candidates in `experiments.html`, `policy-based-evidence.html`, `reading-the-research.html`, and `sessions.html`. Most passed the existing four-question test on inspection (they were doing real, load-bearing contrastive work). **Headings are not exempt from this skill.** Any pass that reviews a piece for this hallmark has to grep and read title and heading text, not just paragraphs, every time.

**Root cause, part two: "not Y" can be individually true and still be the tic, if Y was already said.** The flagged heading sits two items below another heading in the same list that already reads "1974, psychology: 'anchoring and adjustment.'" "Not psychology" is a completely accurate fact, so it passed question 1 of the old four-question test (delete it and you do lose the specific field name). But the reader already has that fact, stated two items up, in the same visual list. Restating it here in contrastive form adds shape, not information the reader doesn't already have. This is a gap the original four questions didn't ask, because they only checked whether a claim was true and specific in isolation, not whether it was new. Question 5 above closes it.

**The fix, in practice.** The heading became "2002: the Nobel goes to economics," letting the body paragraph (which already explains the psychology-versus-economics surprise in full: "a psychology paper about a rigged wheel is also, officially, foundational economics") carry that work instead of front-loading it into the heading a second time. Two more real instances turned up nearby in the same file under the new question 5, both fixed the same way: a "not Monin and Miller's" aside in one `nobody-owns-a-bias.html` list item repeated, one item later, an identical "Not Monin and Miller's" that had already been said; and a closing sentence ("Chronological priority and practical priority aren't the same thing") restated, in the abstract, a contrast the preceding sentence had already made concretely. Both were cut back to say the thing once.

## The 2026-08-25 catch: a labelled two-beat paragraph split reads as stuttered, not direct

The owner flagged the Up Bank teardown on `science-behind.html` directly: "The Up article reads in a stuttered no direct sentences. It's awkward." Root cause: an earlier fix for a different problem (sequential mechanisms reading as disconnected vignettes) had been applied to that one entry by splitting every mechanism into two bolded, labelled paragraphs, `<b>The feature.</b>` then `<b>The mechanism.</b> That's [Principle], ...`, repeated identically seven times in a row, with every mechanism paragraph opening with the same three words. No other entry on the site used this labelled split; the others wove the same bridging-clause fix into one continuous, varied paragraph per mechanism instead, and read fine.

This is a thirteenth hallmark, distinct from the twelve above: **mechanical two-beat paragraph architecture**, a bolded label pair (or a repeated sentence-opener like "That's X") applied identically to every item in a sequence, turning what should be continuous argument into a form with blanks filled in. It's the prose-paragraph version of hallmark #12 (mechanical uniform bullet architecture), and it can slip past the four-question test for hallmark #1 entirely, since each individual "That's X, not Y" or bridging clause can pass on its own; the defect only shows up when the whole sequence is read back to back for rhythm, not sentence by sentence for information content.

**The test.** After writing (or fixing) any piece with more than two sequential sections, blocks, or paragraphs making a parallel argument, read them back to back purely for shape, ignoring content: does every one open with the same words, the same bolded label, or the same sentence construction? A label pair is fine exactly once it's an already-established, skill-documented convention for that specific content type (like `experiment-blueprint`'s `<b>The gap.</b>` inside Theory blocks, used because that skill defines it and every instance still varies its actual content and phrasing around it) and never fine when it's invented on the spot to satisfy a different fix (like a narrative-bridging requirement), because inventing the label is exactly how the mechanical repetition creeps in. Vary the opening, the sentence shape, and where the "reveal" phrase lands, the same discipline hallmark #7 already asks for at the sentence level, applied here at the paragraph-sequence level. See `science-behind-article`'s own "Failure mode: fixing vignettes with a labelled formula creates a new, stuttered problem" for the full worked example and fix.

## The 2026-08-25 catch, part two: the stutter fix itself overcorrected into run-on sentences

The very rewrite that fixed the stuttered two-beat paragraphs above introduced a different, opposite defect in the same piece. Merging each labelled `<b>The feature.</b>` / `<b>The mechanism.</b>` pair into one flowing paragraph was done by joining the two halves with commas and "and," not by keeping them as separate sentences, so several blocks ended up as a single 45-72 word sentence stitched together with 5-8 commas. The owner's catch, verbatim, on the very next round: "The Up write up has sentences too long with too many commas. Doesn't read well." Measured against the site's own four sibling entries (`dual-lip-coffee-cup`, `pay-in-4-checkout`, `goalsaver-bonus-interest`, `japanese-selvedge-denim`), which average 21.3-23.5 words per sentence and a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 10.5-12.0, the merged Up Bank entry measured 30.9 words per sentence and a grade level of 15.1, a full college-level jump above everything else on the page. This is the fourteenth hallmark: **fixing choppiness by fusing short sentences into one long, comma-chained sentence, instead of writing a natural mix of short and medium sentences.** It's the direct opposite failure from hallmark #13 above, and both come from the same root cause: reaching for a single mechanical transformation (always split into two labelled paragraphs; always merge into one paragraph) instead of rewriting for what a specific passage actually needs.

**The check, made quantitative.** "Reads fine to me" is exactly the judgment that missed this twice in a row on the same piece. Before calling a multi-paragraph rewrite finished, count words per sentence and commas per sentence across the whole piece, not just spot-read it. As a rule of thumb tuned to this site's own established voice: average sentence length should land in the low-to-mid 20s (20-25 words), with most sentences under 30 words and under 4 commas; a sentence over 35 words or with 5+ commas is a strong candidate to split, not a stylistic choice to defend. A parallel list genuinely is one sentence even when it runs long (five items separated by commas, all doing the same grammatical job), so judge structure, not just length: if a sentence has two or more independent clauses that could each stand alone, each joined by "and," "so," or a comma splice, split it at those joins rather than trying to shorten it in place.

**The fix, in practice.** Reworked all seven Up Bank blocks a second time, splitting every run-on at its natural clause boundary (an "and" joining two complete thoughts, a colon introducing a second independent clause, a comma splice) back down to the sibling entries' own rhythm: 22.7 words per sentence and grade level 11.9 after the fix, in line with the other four. The same quantitative check, run site-wide, also caught a pre-existing, unrelated instance in `policy-based-evidence.html` (33.3 words per sentence, including one single 101-word sentence built from three semicolon-joined clauses) and several individual run-ons scattered through `principles.html` and `dont-say-the-e-word.html`, none of which came from this session's edits, proof this isn't just a one-off slip on a freshly edited piece but a pattern worth checking on any piece, new or old.

## The 2026-08-29 catch: a working title survives unchecked because it's never the thing being "finished"

**What happened.** The working title for the new backfire special report, "Backfires Aren't One Mistake. They're Five, Wearing the Same Result.," was coined during an early options-review round (confirming the report's thesis angle) and then carried unchanged through three further rounds of options artifacts (placement, then execution) before the report itself had even been written. The owner's catch, on seeing it rendered for the first time inside an execution mockup: "This title reads like ai not like id write." It's a textbook instance of hallmark #1, the classic "It's not X, it's Y" shape (here as "aren't X, they're Y"), and it should have been caught immediately on the four/five-question test. It wasn't, because the test in this skill is written to run "before calling any sentence finished," and a title used only inside chat replies and options-review preview cards, while the actual report file doesn't exist yet, never reaches a moment that reads as "finishing" anything. Each round that reused the title treated it as settled, already-decided text, not new prose to scrutinize, precisely because nothing about repeating a phrase verbatim across rounds feels like writing it.

**The check.** A phrase doesn't need to sit in a shipped file to need this test; it needs the test the moment it's coined, and again every time it's about to be reused verbatim in a new round of anything, chat reply, options-review card, or draft. Titles are the highest-risk case, since a title is read first, read by everyone, and (per the 2026-08-24 catch above) already known to be exactly the kind of text this audit is prone to skip. Before repeating any working title, headline, or pull-quote phrase into a second round of review, run it through the same test as any other sentence, don't grandfather it in as "already decided" just because it survived one round unchallenged.

**The fix, in practice.** Rewrote the title as a plain two-sentence statement with no negation: "Backfires Have Five Different Causes. They All Look the Same." Same information (a typology, one shared symptom), no "X, not Y" reveal-shape to fail the test on. A site-wide grep for the same "[Subject] {isn't/aren't/is not/are not} one/a [X]. {It's/They're} [Y]" title-level shape, across every `<h1 class="principles-index-title">` and `<h3>` on the site, found no other live instance, so this was caught before it could compound, not after a second one shipped.

## The 2026-08-31 catch: the opening sentence of a field session is exactly where this hides best

**What happened.** The Airds High School field session's very first sentence read: "When my own kids ask what I actually do at a bank, the answer that works has never been a sentence. It's a demonstration." That's hallmark #1's classic shape, "has never been X. It's Y," sitting in ordinary narrative prose (not a `.flow-insight` or `.session-quote`, the components this skill already exempts), and it fails the four-question test cleanly: deleting "has never been a sentence" loses no real information, since "the answer that works is always a demonstration" says the identical thing without the manufactured suspense. The owner's catch, verbatim: "the first sentence reads like ai." It survived because opening sentences get written once, early, before the rest of the piece exists to compare rhythm against, and then never get re-read as "new prose" once the piece is otherwise finished, the same blind spot the 2026-08-29 title catch above already documented for headlines, just one level down: not just titles, but any sentence coined early and then treated as settled scaffolding for everything written after it.

**The check.** Read the opening sentence of any new field session, principle article, or report on its own, out loud, specifically after the piece is otherwise done, not just when it was first drafted. An opening line is disproportionately likely to carry this tic because it's the one sentence every writer instinctively tries hardest to make land, and "try hardest to make land" is exactly the impulse that reaches for a manufactured reveal instead of just saying the thing.

**The fix, in practice.** Rewritten to "My own kids sometimes ask what I actually do at a bank, and I've learned the answer that sticks is always a demonstration," a flat positive claim with no negated half, matching the plain, chronological opening style already established on this site's other field sessions (the dentist-visit session opens "I went in for a routine check-up and a filling, mentioned somewhere between the X-ray and the drill what I actually spend my time thinking about," no reveal-shape at all). Checked the rest of the same article for the same construction (found none, the other two "isn't X, it's Y" instances in that piece are a `.session-quote` and a load-bearing real distinction, both legitimate per the existing exceptions) before calling the fix complete.

## The 2026-09-01 catch: a formatting rule scoped to one skill doesn't reach content built by a different one

**What happened.** `behavioural-principle-article` documents a real, twice-caught wall-of-text defect for `.article-block` paragraphs on `principles.html` (split past ~110-120 words, bold 1-2 load-bearing phrases per paragraph, split or not). That rule never got generalized here, so it never reached the live-session tools' reveal narrative, a different content type built by the `live-interactive-session` skill, even though the shape of the problem (dense, unbroken, mobile-width prose with zero visual chunking) is identical. All ten pre-existing `*-live.html` tools shared one unchunked verdict template, and the eleventh (`biased-assimilation-live.html`) reused the same unchunked shape in the same session that had `behavioural-principle-article`'s full text loaded in context, because nothing prompted checking a formatting rule against content a different skill was producing. The owner's catch, on a live screenshot: "The isn't chunked. Lots of content. Root cause and fix and why didn't you use the skills we've built?" See `live-interactive-session`'s own matching entry for the full fix (bold phrases + paragraph split across all eleven tools, plus the `p+p` margin rule the split needed).

**The check.** A writing or formatting rule caught and fixed for one content type is a site-wide rule about how dense prose reads on this site, not a fact about that one file. Before finishing any new content type, or any skill governing one, ask whether an existing skill already solved the same visual shape of problem elsewhere on the site (a wall of text, a missing `<mark>`, a missing dark-mode toggle) and pull that rule forward explicitly, rather than waiting for the identical defect to get flagged a second time in a new location before it counts as "the rule here too."

## Before calling anything finished

Read the piece once, specifically hunting for these fourteen patterns, the way the owner's own global standing policy already asks for a "genuine last read-through from the reader's seat" before calling a document done. This is that read-through, scoped to sentence-level construction rather than document-level scaffolding. Run it on every new principle article, field session, experiment blueprint, and special report as a default step in finishing the piece.

For hallmark #14 specifically, don't rely on the read-through alone: count words and commas per sentence across the piece (a quick script is fine) and check the average against the site's own established range, 20-25 words per sentence, most sentences under 30 words and under 4 commas. This is especially important right after any edit that merges or restructures existing paragraphs, since that's exactly the kind of change that silently pushes sentence length up without the diff looking obviously wrong.

This is a companion to, not a replacement for, the site's standing no-em-dash policy in `CLAUDE.md`. An em dash is one specific punctuation mark; this skill is about sentence-level habits that survive even after every em dash is gone.
