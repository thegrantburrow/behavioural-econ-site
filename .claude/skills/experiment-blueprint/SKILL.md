# Experiment Blueprint

## Why this exists

Digital & Product Experiments is a third content type alongside Principles (reference case studies) and Field Sessions (personal recaps): a testable blueprint for an experiment nobody has run yet, built on top of something the site already established — a principle, a field session, or (best of all) a real cited study. The whole value of that framing is credibility by inheritance: the reader trusts the new experiment because it's visibly standing on real research, not because it sounds plausible. That inheritance breaks in two specific ways, both of which have already happened once on this site and had to be corrected:

1. **Mechanism drift** — the new experiment quietly tests a different kind of decision than the one the source study actually proved.
2. **Over-narrow framing** — the headline experiment gets built around a specific instance of the finding instead of the general case, burying the actually-interesting result under a qualifier nobody asked for.

Both failure modes feel like natural, small steps at the time you write them. That's exactly why they're worth a deliberate check before writing a word.

## Which visual system this covers

An experiment blueprint's app/doc mockup icons (`.app-mock-icon`,
`.doc-mock-icon`), its outcome checklist badges (`.doc-mock-check`), and
its power-calc stat grid (`.power-calc`/`.power-stat`) are their own
distinct systems, shared in part with Field Sessions but not with the
Principles page's eyebrow icons or mechanism diagrams. Before adding a new
icon to an experiment, check the repo's `VISUAL-SYSTEMS.md` for which
system actually applies rather than assuming it's the same one used
elsewhere on the site.

## Failure mode 1: mechanism drift

**What happened.** Buell & Choi's real, cited CBA study (on this site's Tradeoff Transparency principle) is about tradeoff transparency in *product comparison* — showing a credit card's drawbacks as prominently as its perks, when comparing that card against other cards. The first draft of "the next experiment to run" extended this to the credit card's *repayment screen* (minimum payment vs. statement balance vs. fixed amount) — because it's the same product, the same company, and still technically has "options" a customer picks between. But it's a different kind of decision entirely: a single-product payment-method choice, not a multi-product comparison. The site's own tradeoff-transparency mechanism was never tested on that decision, so an experiment built there wasn't really extending the cited study — it was borrowing its credibility for an unrelated idea.

**The check.** Before extending a cited study into a new experiment, write down the exact mechanism the original study tested, in one sentence — not the product, not the company, the *decision type*. ("Showing drawbacks matters when someone is comparing competing options.") Then check the new experiment's decision point against that sentence: is someone still comparing competing options, or have they already committed to one and are now making a different kind of choice (how to pay, how to use, when to cancel)? If it's a different kind of choice, the new experiment needs its own grounding — either a different cited study, or an honest label that this is a hypothesis, not an extension.

**The fix, in practice.** The corrected experiment (`product-comparison-tradeoff-transparency` in `mockup/experiments.html`) stayed inside the same decision type: instead of the repayment screen, it compares a credit card against the *other products* that could finance the same purchase (personal loan, BNPL) — still a product comparison, still the same mechanism, one level up the funnel instead of sideways into a different one. Read that article as the worked example.

## Failure mode 2: over-narrow framing

**What happened.** Halperin, Ho, List & Muir's real Uber study (referenced in the Voltage Effect field session) found two things: a costly, specific apology beats a generic one after a service failure, and — as a sharper secondary nuance — the *identical* apology repeated on a second failure backfires. The first draft of the experiment made the second finding the entire headline: the title, the hypothesis, and both sets of mockups were built around "first failure" vs. "second failure," with the general and much more broadly useful finding (costly-specific beats generic, period) demoted to a supporting detail inside that narrower frame.

**The check.** When a source study has a general finding plus a sharper nested nuance, the general finding is almost always the one that should headline — it's the one that applies to every reader, not just the ones with a repeat-failure workflow. Ask: if I stripped out the narrow qualifier entirely, would the experiment still be worth running and still prove something real? If yes, that's the headline. The narrow case becomes a **sub-branch** — an explicitly labeled secondary section within the same experiment, not a second headline and not a separately recruited test.

**The fix, in practice.** The corrected experiment (`costly-specific-apology` in `mockup/experiments.html`) headlines "a service failure," never states "first failure" (it's implied, not named), and puts the repeat-failure/varied-apology test under its own `<h3>Sub-branch: ...</h3>` heading with a short paragraph explaining it draws from a smaller, non-randomised subset of the same sample rather than a separate recruited group. Read that article as the worked example — including how its `power-calc-note` was rewritten to say "this covers the headline test only" once the repeat-failure caveat moved out of it and into the sub-branch's own text.

## The structural format (established in `mockup/experiments.html`)

Every experiment is an `<article class="experiment" id="some-slug" data-industries="one-or-more-tokens">`, in this order:

1. **Tags** — `.field-note-tag` ("Experiment blueprint"), `.case-tag` ("Digital & Product Experiment"), and `.exp-tags` holding three `.exp-tag` badges: `.industry` (real product area), `.applies` (other businesses this generalises to), `.status` ("Not yet run").
2. **Title + salient-question** — the title states the general hypothesis as a question; the `.salient-question.lg` line (with one or two `<mark>` phrases) is the hook, usually contrasting what's proven against what's still untested.
3. **`.session-meta`** — one line naming exactly what this builds on (a principle, a field session, a cited study), with real links.
4. **`.article-block` sections**, each with a `<span class="k">` label: Theory (name the source study and its exact mechanism, then the gap this experiment fills), Hypothesis, Assumed baseline (always labelled `<b>Illustrative, not real/disclosed data.</b>` — never let an invented number look like a disclosed one), and an Ethical guardrail block when the experiment touches a real financial or wellbeing decision.
   - **Chunk any block that runs long, with a bolded lead-in per chunk.** A Theory (or Hypothesis/Ethics) block that reads as one dense paragraph is hard to read inside the narrow `.rail-content` column on mobile — confirmed directly by a user review round (see `mockup/experiments.html#digital-home-loan-halo` for the worked example). Once a block passes roughly 90–100 words, split it into 2–3 shorter `<p>` tags, each opening with a short bolded lead-in (2–4 words, e.g. `<b>The study.</b>`, `<b>The gap.</b>`, `<b>The prediction.</b>` for Theory; adapt the labels to what the block is actually doing — `<b>The risk.</b>`/`<b>The guardrail.</b>` for an Ethics block, `<b>The hypothesis.</b>`/`<b>What's separate.</b>` for a Hypothesis block with a sub-branch carve-out). Split at natural idea boundaries (the source finding / the gap it leaves / what this experiment does or predicts) — never mid-thought just to hit a word count. This costs nothing structurally: the rail box already scrolls internally, so added height is free. Below that word count, leave it as a single paragraph — don't chunk for its own sake.
5. **Control vs. treatment mockups** — `.app-mock-grid` (2 columns) or `.app-mock-grid.cols-3` (3 columns) holding `.app-mock-col` > `.app-mock-phone` > `.app-mock-screen` blocks. For a multi-item pros/cons comparison inside one screen (not separate arms), reuse `.method-grid` with `.method-col.strength`/`.method-col.weakness` rather than inventing a new comparison layout. A `.app-mock-callout` is available for an inline cost/fact callout; `.app-chip.selected` marks the pre-selected option in a set of `.app-chip`s.
6. **`.power-calc`** — a `.power-calc-grid` of `.power-stat` cells (baseline, target, significance, power, sample per arm, total sample, assumed volume, estimated run time) plus a `.power-calc-note` stating the actual formula used and flagging which numbers are assumed vs. calculated. Compute real numbers — don't eyeball a sample size. For a proportion outcome: n = (z<sub>α/2</sub> + z<sub>β</sub>)² × [p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂)] / (p₁−p₂)². For a continuous outcome (e.g. a 1–5 CSAT score): n = 2 × (z<sub>α/2</sub> + z<sub>β</sub>)² × σ² / (μ₁−μ₂)². Use z<sub>0.025</sub> = 1.96, z<sub>0.80</sub> = 0.84 for the standard 95%/80%-power setup unless there's a reason to vary it. Pick an assumed volume and derive a run-time estimate from it — that's what makes the sample size feel like a real plan instead of a stats-class exercise.
7. **`.doc-mock` outcome variables** — a checklist tagged `<b>Primary:</b>`, `<b>Secondary:</b>`, and `<b>Guardrail:</b>` per item. Always include at least one guardrail that could catch the experiment doing real harm (activation dropping, missed payments rising, complaints increasing) — this site's whole ethos (see `index.html#ethics-of-not-experimenting`) is measuring honestly, not just chasing the primary metric.
   - **Markup for each item**: `<li><span class="doc-mock-check" aria-hidden="true">&check;</span><span><b>Primary:</b><br>description text</span></li>` — the tag and its description **must** be wrapped together in one `<span>`. `.doc-mock-list li` is a flex row, and any element child of a flex container becomes its own flex item regardless of its normal display type — an unwrapped `<b>` sits *beside* the following text as a second flex item in the same row instead of stacking above it, which silently squeezes the description into a narrower column than it should have (this exact bug shipped once and was fixed by wrapping; don't reintroduce it). The wrapping `<span>` is what makes the tag-then-`<br>`-then-description stack correctly and gives the description the wrapper's full width.
   - **Mobile real estate is tight here on purpose — don't assume desktop-width comfort.** `.rail-content` inside `.rail-scroll` is narrow by design (the rail-nav column, though small, is permanent at every viewport), and `.doc-mock`'s own padding plus the checklist's checkbox further reduce it. On a ~390px phone the checklist text column is roughly 215px wide even after the fixes below — write outcome-variable descriptions with that in mind (they already tend to be one dense clause; that's the right length, don't pad them out). If a future change to this component is needed, the existing widths were set deliberately after measuring actual wrapped-line counts on a real mobile viewport (see the `f06206b` commit in `mockup/styles.css`) — verify any change against a 390px screenshot, not just desktop, before shipping it.
8. **Closing `.also-note`** linking back to the Principles page mechanism(s) this experiment is built on.

## Ask about real screenshots before drafting, if a real product gets named

If the Theory block is going to build on a real, specific product or company's actual screen (not a hypothetical control/treatment mockup, an existing real thing the experiment extends, the way `costly-specific-apology` extends the Uber apology flow), ask the owner up front whether he has real screenshots of it, per `spotted-in-the-wild`'s "Step 0" rule. This is separate from the invented `.app-mock-screen` control/treatment mockups every experiment already builds (those stay invented, illustrating the proposed test, not real evidence), it only applies when the Theory section cites something real that already exists and could be shown, not proposed.

## Where the supporting pieces live

- `mockup/experiments.html` — the page itself; append new `<article>`s after the existing ones, separated by `<hr class="rule">`, and add a matching `<li>` to the `.session-toc` list and a matching filter chip / count in `#experimentsFilterTabs` if it's a new product area.
- `mockup/styles.css` — the `/* ---------- digital & product experiments page ---------- */` block has every class listed above already built. Reuse them; don't add new CSS unless a genuinely new visual need comes up (as `.app-mock-callout` and `.app-chip.selected` did).
- `mockup/script.js` — the experiments filter IIFE matches `#experimentsFilterTabs` against `article.experiment[data-industries]` (space-separated tokens, "any match" not exact match). No changes needed for a new experiment unless it needs a new product-area token.
- `mockup/search-index.js` — add an entry under the Experiments section so the new blueprint is reachable from the site's own search boxes, not just its own page's filter. Check the blurb actually contains the experiment's own defining word: the named principle it's built on (e.g. "Halo Effect", "Pseudo-Set Framing"), any acronym central to it (e.g. "BNPL"), not just a paraphrase of the mechanism. This is one instance of a site-wide requirement — see `CLAUDE.md`'s "every new or edited page keeps `search-index.js` in sync" standing policy for the full failure mode (found live on 2026-09-04: four pre-existing experiments had shipped without ever getting a `search-index.js` row) and the diff-the-ids check to run before calling any new experiment finished.

## Before publishing, check both failure modes explicitly

Once a draft is written, reread it and ask the two questions above in order: (1) can I state the source study's mechanism in one sentence, and does this experiment's decision point match it — same *type* of decision, not just same product? (2) if I stripped out every qualifier and edge case, is the remaining headline still the interesting, general result — or did I bury it under a narrower one? If either answer is no, fix the structure before fixing the prose.

Also run `python3 scripts/check_inline_references.py` from the repo root before calling it finished: it catches a real, common failure where the principle or Science Behind entry an experiment is built on gets a link in `session-meta` and the closing `also-note`, but never at the actual point in the Theory/Hypothesis prose where it's named. See `CLAUDE.md`'s "every cross-page reference gets inline-linked in body prose, not just meta" standing policy.

## Don't bolt on a Special Report's story-stack or evidence-at-a-glance

Special Reports use two chunked, icon-led summary components (a narrative `.story-stack` and, mandatory past 2 cited studies, a second one labeled "The evidence at a glance" with headline real numbers) — see the `special-report` skill. A 2026-08-27 audit checked whether Experiments needed the same treatment and found `.rail-nav` (Theory, Hypothesis, Baseline, Ethics, Design, Sample size, Outcomes) already gives a reader the shape of the blueprint at a glance, and per-experiment citation counts run low (most cite exactly the one study they're built on, in the Theory block). Note too: this content type reuses the `.case-cite` CSS class for plain "Read the full blueprint &rsaquo;" cross-links between experiments, which is not the same thing as a real academic citation, don't count those toward the threshold below.

**The rule, going forward:** don't add a story-stack or an evidence-at-a-glance stack to an experiment by default. Only add a compact evidence summary if a specific experiment's Theory/Baseline blocks cite more than 2 real academic studies (rare, checked site-wide and found in zero of the 21 live experiments at the time of the audit), and build it in this content type's own visual language rather than copy-pasting the Special Report component verbatim.

## Never write an em dash

Not `—`, not `&mdash;`, not `&#8212;`. Use a period, colon, comma, or parentheses depending on what the sentence is actually doing (see `CLAUDE.md`'s standing policy). This was a real defect fixed across the whole site once already; don't let it come back in new experiment write-ups.

## Designing treatment copy through iterative review, not upfront perfection

**What happened.** A confirmation-screen prompt experiment for Implementation Intentions was built with four candidate treatments: a vivid cue, a self-built if-then sentence, naming the obstacle in advance (goal shielding), and a passive text-only suggestion. Two were approved on first review. The other two came back marked "Needs work" with comments that read as vague on their face: one said the auto-filled coping response "just feels like prioritising this over something important," the other said a passive suggestion "feels like back imposing and be directive and arrogant." Neither comment named a specific line to fix. Digging into *why* each one landed that way (not just rewording the flagged sentence) surfaced the same root cause both times: the copy was written from the bank's own goal (get the customer to show up) instead of the customer's actual benefit, and in one case it went further, presuming the bank's call outranked whatever the customer might already be doing. Once that root cause was named, both fixes were specific and fast: rewrite the auto-filled response so it never tells the customer to abandon something for the bank's sake, and reframe the passive suggestion as optional and attributed to other customers rather than prescribed by the bank. Both treatments were approved on the very next round, and this exact skill section didn't exist until after that experiment shipped, an oversight this entry corrects.

**The process, made repeatable.**
1. **Build every candidate treatment as a real, rendered option**, not a description, using the `design-options-review` skill's `oscarfinch-feedback-html` template (binary Looks good / Needs work toggle, a comment box, a live-compiling feedback panel). Don't hand-pick a "best" option and skip the review step just because a treatment feels obviously right, the two that got flagged in the case above looked reasonable on first write.
2. **Treat a vague "needs work" comment as a diagnostic clue, not a rewrite instruction.** A short comment like "feels arrogant" or "prioritising this over something important" rarely names the exact fix. Ask what psychological stance the copy is actually taking: whose goal is centred, the business's or the customer's? Does any line presume the customer's priorities, time, or attention belongs to the company by default? The fix usually falls out once that diagnosis is made explicit, rewriting words without first answering that question tends to produce a second, differently-worded version of the same underlying problem.
3. **Republish the same review artifact in place** (same URL, updated content, a version note describing what changed and why) rather than starting a fresh one, so the reviewer can track what changed round to round without re-orienting.
4. **Only promote an option into a real experiment arm once it's actually approved.** A "needs work" treatment with an unresolved tone problem has no business becoming Treatment C in a live blueprint just because four treatments makes a rounder set than two.
5. **When treatments need to work across more than one real-world context** (e.g. the same four prompts adapted for a phone appointment and an in-branch appointment), design and get each treatment approved once, in its primary context, before adapting it to the second context. Adapting an approved, root-cause-fixed treatment to a new context is a much smaller, lower-risk edit than designing and fixing tone problems in both contexts simultaneously.

**Applying it to a new mechanism.** This process is mechanism-agnostic: it was developed for Implementation Intentions treatments, and the same five steps apply to designing Operational Transparency treatments, or any future principle's set of candidate treatments. Build the candidates as real rendered options first, get binary feedback, diagnose root causes behind any "needs work" comment rather than patching the flagged phrase, republish in place until approved, then promote to a real experiment arm.
