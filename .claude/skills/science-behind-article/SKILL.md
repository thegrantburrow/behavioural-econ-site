# Science Behind Article

## Why this exists

"The Science Behind" was approved 2026-08-23 after a 4-decision options review (structure, categories, nav, homepage placement). The owner's own instruction on picking the winning structure: "Use this as the base but enrich with option b and c which add value. But don't overwhelm. Be elegant... You'll need to codify as a skill so you can produce these as a standard for each new entry." This file is that codification. Every future entry should read like it came from the same hand as the first one, not like each was designed from scratch.

## What this content type is, and isn't

It starts from a real, recognisable object or practice, not a study. The mechanisms cited are the site's own Principles, linked back to their full citation and study-card on `principles.html`, never re-argued from scratch here. This piece's job is to connect a real thing to mechanisms the site has already earned the right to cite, not to introduce new academic claims. If a genuinely new mechanism is needed and it isn't on the Principles page yet, write that principle first (`behavioural-principle-article` skill), then write the Science Behind entry that cites it.

## Failure mode: reaching for whatever principle already exists, instead of the one that's actually true

**What happened.** The two flagship entries (selvedge denim, the dual-lip coffee cup) both did the thing this file already warned against in the paragraph above, in practice, not just in theory. The coffee cup entry cited Halo Effect three separate times, for the cup's rim-thickness cue changing tactile perception, the mug-colour cue changing visual perception, and the general idea of a vessel cue standing in for a taste judgment. None of those are Halo Effect: that principle is specifically "one trait quietly colours belief about a *different, unrelated* trait of the same source, that nobody actually checked." A cup rim changing how sweet the same liquid tastes isn't an unverified belief about a separate trait, it's a direct sensory effect, real crossmodal perception research (Van Doorn, Wuillemin & Spence 2014; Ichimura et al. 2023), that had no principle on the site yet. Separately, the denim entry cited Signpost Effect for "a visible selvedge edge reads as a quality signal," but Signpost Effect is specifically about *reframing an already-meaningful fact* to activate a dormant goal (a car's fuel economy shown as MPG vs. a greenhouse-gas rating); a selvedge edge isn't a reframed fact, it's a costly, hard-to-fake detail whose mere presence gets read as proof of an unstated benefit, the real, differently-named phenomenon Carpenter, Glazer & Nakamoto (1994) documented. The owner's catch, verbatim, circling the mismatched chips on both live entries: "These seem limited by content on the site."

**The check.** Before picking which principle chip to attach to a mechanism, do the reverse of the usual process: don't start from the site's existing Principles list and ask "which of these is closest." Start from the real-world phenomenon itself (why does this cup actually change taste? why does this edge actually read as quality?), research it properly (WebSearch for the actual academic field this sits in, not just the closest site content), and only then check whether an existing Principle genuinely is that mechanism, tested against its own definition line on `principles.html`, not just its vibe. A principle "sort of" fitting because it's in the neighbourhood (both are about impressions, both are about signals) is exactly the failure that happened twice here.

**The fix, in practice.** Two new principles were researched and written from scratch, each with its own real seminal study: Crossmodal Correspondence (Van Doorn, Wuillemin & Spence, 2014, `principles.html#crossmodal-correspondence`) and Meaningless Differentiation (Carpenter, Glazer & Nakamoto, 1994, `principles.html#meaningless-differentiation`). The two Science Behind entries were then corrected to cite the accurate principle instead of the closest available one. Where an existing principle genuinely was the right fit (Halo Effect for the denim entry's "loom provenance implies untested durability" claim, IKEA Effect for its break-in-through-wear claim, Scarcity for its real shrinking-production-capacity claim), those stayed exactly as they were: this isn't a rule to always write a new principle, it's a rule to verify the fit is real before reusing what's already there.

## Failure mode: decoding a category or pattern instead of one real product

**What happened.** The "Pay in 4" checkout entry was written about a generic composite: "a checkout screen offering '4 interest-free payments of $25.00' instead of one $100 charge," with no single named product anywhere in the title, lead, hero icon, or mechanism blocks, only three competing brands (Afterpay, Klarna, Affirm) listed at the very bottom in the Real Examples block. The owner's catch, verbatim, circling the title: "There is no clear product. These MUST have a clear product with reference to the product." He's right: this entry never actually decoded a real thing, it decoded an industry-wide pattern and borrowed the credibility of naming real brands only at the point furthest from the actual claims. The two flagship entries didn't have this problem because their subject (raw selvedge denim, a specific dual-lip porcelain cup model) was already a single, concrete, literal object, not a category with several different real implementations underneath it.

**The check.** Before writing the header, lead, or a single mechanism block, ask: is the thing being decoded one specific real product I could point to, screenshot, or hold, or is it a pattern that several different competing products each implement slightly differently? A UI pattern (a payment-plan progress bar, a pricing-tier ladder, a checkout flow) is a category, not a product, exactly the same distinction `experiment-blueprint`'s mechanism-drift check draws between a decision type and a specific case of it. If the answer is "a pattern," stop and pick the single most representative real product to decode, then verify its actual, specific, currently documented behaviour (its real schedule, its real fee structure, its real copy) before writing a word, the same research discipline this skill already requires for a craft object's production history.

**The fix, in practice.** The entry was rewritten around Afterpay's actual "Pay in 4" feature specifically, not the BNPL category in general: the title, lead, hero icon, and every mechanism block name Afterpay and describe its real, publicly documented terms (four payments every two weeks, roughly 25% due at purchase, interest-free, fees only for a missed payment). Klarna and Affirm moved to the closing Real Examples block as real alternative implementations of the same pattern, exactly where system rule 6 below already says brand examples belong, rather than substituting for a named product everywhere else in the piece. The general rule this establishes: **the header, lead, and every mechanism block must name and describe one specific real product's actual behaviour throughout, never a generic composite of "a checkout that does X."** Other real products belong only in the closing Real Examples section as illustrative alternatives, never as a stand-in for naming the one product actually being decoded.

## The structure, in this order

The winning direction merged three reviewed options into one: sequential mechanism prose (readable, does the actual explaining), a quick-scan chip strip (lets a skimmer get the gist in five seconds), and a closing real-vs-perceived split (the site's honesty instinct, made explicit). Every entry gets all three, in this order, and nothing else layered on top, that combination was explicitly chosen over piling on more structure ("don't overwhelm, be elegant").

1. **Header.** A large hero icon (system #18, see below) beside the entry's title. Title is a question, matching the site's `salient-question` convention elsewhere: name the surprising thing, not the product category ("Why does raw selvedge denim feel like better quality than jeans that actually fit?" not "About Selvedge Denim"). One `.sb-lead` sentence under the title stating the object plainly.
2. **Chip strip** (`.sb-chips`). One `.sb-chip` per mechanism used in this entry, 3-4 typical, each linking straight to that principle's anchor on `principles.html`. This is the five-second version of the whole piece; a reader should be able to stop here and already have the gist.
3. **Mechanism walkthrough** (`.sb-block`, one per mechanism, same order as the chip strip). Each block names the mechanism, explains the plain mechanism (not a repeat of the principle's own copy, write it fresh for this specific object), and points at the literal, physical detail of the real thing that triggers it. This is the actual explaining; the chips are a index into it, not a substitute for it.
4. **Real vs. Perceived split** (`.sb-split`, two columns). Left column: what's genuinely, physically true about the object, verifiable, would still be true with all the marketing stripped away. Right column: the psychological framing layered on top, each item linked to its mechanism. This is the capstone, not a repeat of section 3, keep each line to a single clause.
5. **Sources.** A `.sb-sources` block. Every mechanism cited already has its full citation on the Principles page, don't re-cite it here, just confirm the link target is correct. Any claim about the *object itself* (a production process, a material fact, a piece of industry history) that isn't common knowledge needs its own real source, a real article or industry reference, not a fabricated one. If a fact can't be verified, say so or cut it, the same rule the whole site already runs on.
6. **Real examples** (`.sb-brands` block). Real, currently operating brands or products, with real links to their actual sites, spanning a real price range where price is relevant. Never invent a brand, a price, or a URL. WebSearch to confirm a brand's site is real and current before linking it; if a specific price can't be confirmed with confidence, describe a range sourced from more than one real listing rather than pinning a single number that might already be stale. These are illustrative real examples, not endorsements or affiliate placements, no tracking parameters, no sponsored framing.

## The icon (system #18 in `VISUAL-SYSTEMS.md`)

Every entry gets one hero icon, `.sb-hero-icon`, sitting **nested inline beside the title in a row at every viewport width** (icon box 72px on mobile, 96px from 600px up). The same drawing repeats at the small `.beats-icon` scale in that entry's homepage teaser card, with a touch more stroke weight (~1.3) since fine detail is lost at that size.

It must depict the *literal* real object, a jean leg with a visible selvedge cuff for denim, a coffee cup with its actual rim geometry, a ballot for political messaging, never a generic category stand-in (no generic "shirt" icon for a specific denim weave, the same literal-object discipline `principle-mechanism-diagram` already enforces on illustrations).

**Check the silhouette against the real object's actual proportions before finalizing**, not just whether it has enough detail. A cup's wall should bow outward like a real vessel, not run as two parallel straight lines to a flat U-bottom; a leg should taper the way a real leg does, not read as a uniform tube. This is a real, corrected mistake (see history below), not a hypothetical one.

Add 2-4 small, authentic surface details specific to the real object, the things it actually has, not decoration invented to fill space: a stitched pocket and its rivets for denim, a thin/thick rim split for a dual-lip cup, a pressure gauge or steam-wand for an espresso machine. Each detail should use a different site palette color doing real work, not one flat accent: terracotta for a structural edge or seam, mustard for a stitch line or secondary edge, teal for small hardware or a dimple mark. A thick accent representing a genuinely thick real edge (a "thick lip" rim) needs `stroke-linecap="butt"`, not the icon's default `round`, or it renders as a blobby pill instead of a line. Ink stays the main outline; stroke-width is **1.1-1.3**, thinner than the standard 1.6 tag icon, so the mark reads as refined rather than heavy. 24x24 viewBox still.

**Realistic-proportion checklist, run this before ever wiring a new icon in.** A thin stroke and a realistic silhouette are two separate things to get right, not one (see history below for exactly how that was learned). Sketch the base silhouette alone first, with no detail on it, and check it against these before adding pocket/rim/rivet detail on top:

- **No dimension exaggerated more than ~40% past its neighbour.** A hip flare, a bulge, a taper, whichever the object has, keep it inside a 15-40% swing from the adjacent measurement (waist to hip, neck to shoulder, whatever the object's actual anatomy is). A first denim pass at a 62% waist-to-hip flare read as a diaper, not jeans, even after the outline itself was already thinned.
- **Sharp transitions stay sharp; round ones stay round, match the real object, don't default to a smooth curve everywhere.** A garment's crotch is a V, not a U. A vessel's wall is a continuous bow, not two straight parallel lines meeting a flat bottom. Pick the curve family that matches the specific real object, not whichever bezier is easiest to type.
- **Long thin things should look long and thin.** A leg, a handle, a spout: check the length-to-width ratio against the real object (roughly 5:1 for a slim jean leg at the ankle, for instance) rather than a stubby ~1.5:1 that reads as a cartoon limb.
- **A thick accent line is a butt-capped rectangle, not a round-capped pill.** Any accent meant to read as a thick physical edge (a thick cup lip, a wide seam) needs `stroke-linecap="butt"` specifically on that path, independent of the icon's overall linecap setting.
- **Screenshot the bare silhouette next to a mental (or real) reference image of the object before adding any detail.** Judge the outline alone first; a pocket and rivets on top of a wrong silhouette just makes a wrong silhouette with pockets on it.

Before drawing one, look at what's actually distinctive about the object, not what's easiest to draw. Prototype it standalone at the real display size (Playwright screenshot, not a thumbnail glance) before wiring it into the page, the same discipline `principle-mechanism-diagram` requires for illustrations, since detail that looks fine at 24px can still read as clutter or as an unrelated shape once scaled up (a first pass at this size accidentally read as a two-legged figure with a head before the waistband tab and hem ticks were cut).

**Revision history, read before reaching for "bigger and bolder" again.** The icon shipped 2026-08-22 at ~2x eyebrow scale, got bumped same day to ~3.5-4x scale with stroke-width 1.7-1.8 and a full-width stacked-above-title mobile layout, on direct feedback asking for exactly that: "3-4 times bigger... thicker outline... use all of the site palette colours." That heavier version shipped on two entries (denim, coffee cup) and then got the opposite correction on 2026-08-23: "Icons here need to be more realistically proportioned. Outline too thick. Should be nest with header." A 3-option layout review (nested inline / centred / corner badge) was built and reviewed before landing on nested inline and thinner 1.1-1.3 strokes on both icons. The first pass at "realistic" still wasn't: the denim silhouette kept a ~62% waist-to-hip flare and a smooth rounded crotch curve, which read as a cartoon/diaper shape even at the thinner stroke, per the follow-up catch ("This but the jeans look like a cartoon. Proportions should be very realistic as iconography."). The fix was a full silhouette rebuild against real garment proportions, not just a thinner line: waist and hip within ~15-40% of each other (not 62%), a sharp V-shaped crotch apex instead of a smooth U, and slender legs (roughly 5:1 length-to-width at the ankle, not ~1.4:1). Neither the "bigger and bolder" instruction nor "thinner and realistic" was wrong in isolation, each genuinely fixed what came before it, but "thinner" and "actually proportioned like the real object" turned out to be two separate fixes, not one. Don't treat this file's current numbers as more final than the ones they replaced: prototype, ship, and look again.

## The `.illustration` mechanism diagram, when an entry needs one

Some entries need more than the hero icon to show a finding, the dual-lip-coffee-cup entry's "the rim" and "the glaze" sections each carry a full `.illustration bg-*` box (system #2 in `VISUAL-SYSTEMS.md`) comparing two conditions of the same real study. This is the *exact same component* `principle-mechanism-diagram` governs on the Principles page, not a Science-Behind-specific variant, load that skill and follow its layout discipline to the letter: a single centered column, title, a small recognizable icon (never a `<rect fill="rgba(255,255,255,0.08)">` or any other giant background panel wrapping a row), a bold label, an arrow, a bold outcome line, a dashed divider, the mirrored row, and a final italic caption. `principle-mechanism-diagram`'s failure mode 20 documents exactly this component regressing to a bulkier panel-box layout on this page (both dual-lip-coffee-cup diagrams shipped that way once and had to be rebuilt), precisely because this cross-reference didn't originally call out the *diagram's own layout*, only the hero icon's literal-object discipline (above). If a future Science Behind entry needs an `.illustration`, open one of the pilot diagrams (`principles.html#loss-aversion`, `#halo-effect`, `#goal-gradient`) as the literal template before drawing anything, the same way a new icon should be prototyped against the realistic-proportion checklist above.

## Categories: two axes, both shown, both browsable

The review explicitly asked to combine the domain taxonomy and the signal-type taxonomy rather than pick one, "keep distinct for easy navigation and categorisation as we scale and to show where it fits in each piece." Every entry carries both:

- **Domain** (`data-domain`): the primary navigation axis, using the same landing-hub category-tile pattern (system #17) as Principles/Sessions/Experiments. Current domains, in the order they appear in `science-behind.html`'s tile grid: `craft-materials` (Craft & Materials), `food-drink` (Food & Drink), `tech-product` (Tech & Product Design), `money-banking` (Money & Banking). Add a new domain only when an entry genuinely doesn't fit an existing one, don't split an existing domain just to keep counts even. The tile grid only has four `.tile-icon-chip` colour variants defined (`chip-a`/`chip-b`/`chip-c`/`chip-d`, added when the fourth domain shipped); reuse one only once all four are already taken by other domains, and add a fifth CSS variant before reaching for that, rather than repeating a colour while spare variants exist.
- **Signal type** (`data-signal`): a secondary `.toc-tabs` filter row beneath the domain tiles, cutting across domains. Current signals: `looks-handmade` (What Looks Handmade), `feels-premium` (What Feels Premium), `feels-affordable` (What Feels Affordable), `feels-protected` (What Feels Protected), `feels-effortless` (What Feels Effortless). This list drifts out of date each time a new signal ships (it listed `sounds-true` and `feels-personal`, neither of which any live entry actually used, while missing others that did) — before writing a new entry, verify this list against `science-behind.html`'s own `#sbSignalTabs` markup directly rather than trusting this doc alone.

Both tags render as visible chips on the entry itself (`.sb-tag.domain` and `.sb-tag.signal`), not just in the filter UI, so a reader landing on one entry can see where it sits on both axes without going back to the hub.

## The landing-hub title never states a raw count

**What happened.** With only 2 entries live, `.landing-hub-title` read "2 real things, decoded so far", the same count-in-title pattern Principles ("66 principles...") and Experiments ("16 blueprints...") use. At a small count that pattern undersells the page instead of establishing it. The owner's catch: "Dont reference how many write ups in the title." Fixed to "Real products, decoded into the mechanism", reusing the exact phrase already established in this page's own nav description (`science-behind.html`'s `nav-desc`) rather than inventing new wording.

**The fix, in practice.** Never put the live entry count in `.landing-hub-title`. Write the title as a standing identity statement that reads the same whether there are 2 entries or 50. The count itself still belongs somewhere, just on a control, not in a headline claim: the `All N` chip and the `cat-tile` counts (see the merge below for exactly where that landed once the second heading was removed).

## At low entry counts, merge the hub and the index into one section

**What happened.** Once the chip and list-row icons above made the page's structure more visible, the underlying duplication became obvious: the landing-hub's category tiles and the search section's "By category" chips directly below them showed the identical 2 categories, with the identical icons and counts, and both sections opened with their own "THE SCIENCE BEHIND" eyebrow and a near-identical heading ("Real products, decoded into the mechanism" then "What's really behind it" a few lines later). The owner's catch: "These menus looked quite duplicated now? Evaluate and give options." This two-section shape (a category-tile hub, then a full search/filter/list index) is shared with Principles, Field Sessions, and Experiments, and reads fine there because those pages have enough content that the hub is a genuinely different, faster tool than the full index below it. At 2 entries split into 2 categories, both sections were doing the exact same job.

**The check.** Before shipping a new hub + index pair for this page (or judging whether an existing one still earns its two-section shape), count what's actually different between them. If the hub's tiles and the index's category chips would show the identical set of categories with identical counts, and the hub has no meaningfully separate content of its own (no larger preview, no additional metadata), the second category control isn't adding a tool, it's restating the first one.

**The fix, in practice.** Three options were built and screenshotted for real (removing the hub entirely, keeping both but deleting just the repeated chip row, merging into one section) and reviewed side by side; the owner picked the merge. `science-behind.html` now has a single `<section class="landing-hub" id="hub"><div class="wrap" id="top">` holding, in order: the eyebrow, the one heading and intro, the category tiles (now the sole domain filter, not just a navigation shortcut into a separate control), a divider, the search box, the signal chips, and the full list. There is exactly one heading, one intro, one category control, for the whole page.

- **The category tiles became the real filter**, not a proxy that clicks a hidden duplicate control. `.cat-tile` buttons carry `data-filter-target` (matching the `data-domain` values on `article.sb-entry`) and a shared `.sb-domain-btn` class alongside a standalone `.toc-tab.sb-domain-btn` "All" pill sitting above the tile grid, reusing the existing pill look rather than inventing a third control style for the reset state. `script.js`'s Science Behind filter IIFE queries `.sb-domain-btn` directly (was `#sbDomainTabs .toc-tab`, which no longer exists) and toggles `aria-pressed` (not `aria-selected`, since these buttons aren't a `role="tab"` group any more) alongside the existing `.active` class.
- **The generic cross-page hub script must not also wire these tiles.** The shared `.landing-hub` click handler (used by Principles/Sessions/Experiments to proxy a cat-tile click into a hidden real toc-tab elsewhere on the page) would otherwise attach a second, redundant listener to these same tiles. It now bails out early for any hub containing `.sb-domain-btn`, with a comment explaining why, rather than leaving two competing click handlers on the same button.
- **A merged tile row that's exactly 2 (or any small fixed number) needs its own column count, not the shared 3-up desktop grid.** The site-wide `.cat-grid` defaults to 3 columns on desktop; left alone, 2 tiles leave an empty third track and look unfinished, especially now that the tiles are the page's only category control instead of a secondary shortcut. Added `.cat-grid.cols-2 { grid-template-columns: repeat(2, 1fr); max-width: 460px; }`, scoped to this page only, so the two tiles sit in one tidy row at any width instead of stretching edge to edge or leaving a gap. `.cat-tile.active` (a terracotta border and a faint terracotta tint) gives the selected tile a real pressed state, since it's now a persistent filter, not a one-shot navigation button.
- **This is a per-page judgement call, not a new default.** Principles, Field Sessions, and Experiments keep their two-section shape; they have enough entries per category that the hub is still a faster, different tool than the full index. Re-run the check above before merging any of them, rather than assuming this fix generalises just because it shipped here.

## Chips and list rows: reuse the entry's real icon, but simplify it for small sizes

**What happened.** The `.toc-tabs` category/signal filter chips and the `.session-toc` list originally showed plain text only, no icon, on every row and chip. The owner's catch: "The iconography for each piece should be in the right clickable rows of them eg the jeans in line and left of the selvage jeans. Same with cup for its respective piece." A first attempt reused the entry's full `.sb-hero-icon` markup (drawn for 50-96px display) directly at list-row scale (~19px) and at chip scale (~14px): the denim icon's thin selvedge accent lines and the coffee cup's thin rim-thickness accent both disappeared into illegible noise at that size, confirmed by an actual Playwright screenshot at real display size, not a spec-level assumption that "the same SVG will just scale down fine."

**The check.** A detailed icon drawn for 50px+ display (per the icon system above, 2-4 accent-colored surface details, 1.1-1.3 stroke) is a different artifact from what belongs at chip or list-row scale, not a smaller render of the same file. Before wiring any icon into a `.toc-tab-icon` (14px), `.hub-anchor-icon` (15px), or `.session-toc-icon` (19px) slot, screenshot it at that exact real display size the same way the hero icon itself gets checked. If any accent line or fine internal detail is unreadable at that size, drop it, don't shrink it.

**The fix, in practice.** For chip and list-row scale, use only the entry's main body silhouette path, at a bumped stroke-width (1.6, not the hero icon's 1.1-1.3) and with the accent-colored fine detail lines removed entirely, not just recolored or thinned further. The object must still be the same one used in the entry's own `.sb-hero-icon`, a reader should recognise it as "the same jeans icon, smaller," never a different or generic mark, just simplified to survive the size. Category-level chips (the domain axis: Craft & Materials, Food & Drink) already had their own simpler `.cat-icon` from the landing-hub tile system (system #17), reuse that directly rather than simplifying the object icon a second way, one simplified icon per category, not two competing ones. Signal-axis chips (What Looks Handmade, What Feels Premium, and future signals) have no existing icon to reuse, so design a new small, literal icon per signal the first time it's used (a needle-and-thread for "looks handmade," a faceted gem for "feels premium") and reuse that exact icon everywhere that signal appears from then on, the same one-icon-per-concept discipline as the object icons.

The list-row markup wraps the icon in its own box (`.session-toc-icon`, additive CSS keyed to a `.has-icon` class so Field Sessions and Experiments, which share the base `.session-toc`/`.toc-tabs` classes but don't yet have a matching per-row icon source, render exactly as before). Since the hub and index merged into one section (below), there's now only the one `#sbToc` list to add the icon to, not two.

## Voice and rigor, same as everywhere else on the site

- **No em dashes**, anywhere. See `CLAUDE.md`'s standing policy, real punctuation only.
- **Run the `authentic-voice` four-question test** before calling any entry finished, the contrastive "X, not Y" tic is exactly as easy to fall into here as anywhere else on the site.
- **Never fabricate a URL.** A brand link, a source link, an icon reference, every one has to be a real, verifiable address. If a fact can't be verified, disclose that rather than guessing.
- **The tone stays descriptive, not preachy.** This is decoding, not shaming a product or its buyers for responding to real psychology. The `behavioural-principle-article` skill's rule (teach recognition, don't hand out a manipulation playbook) applies here too, especially in the real-vs-perceived split: the perceived column names a real mechanism, it doesn't imply the buyer was foolish for responding to it.
