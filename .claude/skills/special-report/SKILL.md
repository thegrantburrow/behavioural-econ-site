# Special Report

## Why this exists

By 2026-08-24 this site had shipped eight special reports (`evidence-strength.html`, `policy-based-evidence.html`, `dont-say-the-e-word.html`, `how-you-pay-changes-what-you-spend.html`, `small-sample-big-claim.html`, `savings-lottery.html`, `nobody-owns-a-bias.html`, `six-moves-behind-every-pricing-page.html`) all following the same unwritten structure, plus a ninth (`not-testing-is-still-a-bet.html`) that broke one part of it. Nobody had written the structure down. This file does that, so the next report matches the established shape on purpose instead of by luck.

## What this content type is, and isn't

It starts from an argument: a thesis the site is making, built by tying two or more real, cited findings (academic papers, and where relevant real narrative sources like a podcast episode) together under one claim. It's not a single principle's study card (that's `behavioural-principle-article`), not a future test (`experiment-blueprint`), and not a real object decoded (`science-behind-article`). A real narrative source (a podcast, a documented public statement) can carry the story, but the load-bearing citation is still the real academic paper behind it, labeled separately, exactly as `dont-say-the-e-word.html` labels List's own peer-reviewed paper as the citation and his public post as "restated publicly."

## The structure, in this order

1. **Header block** (`<section class="principles-index" id="top">`): `<span class="label">Special Report</span>`, an `<h1 class="principles-index-title">` stating the thesis as a title (a chiasmus, a plain declarative, or a named argument, never a category label), then the intro (see the chunking rule below), then a `<p class="story-stack-label">The story in four parts</p>` and a `.story-stack` of exactly four `.story-stack-item`s (icon + `<h4>` + one-sentence `<p>`), each beat moving the argument forward, not restating the same point four times.
2. **Report body** (`<section class="report report-light" id="...">`): a sequence of `.report-section` blocks, each with its own `<h3>` and one or more `<p class="intro">` paragraphs. A section presenting a real case usually uses `.case-grid` of `.case-card`s (tag, `<h4>`, `.salient-question` with a `<mark>`, `.case-body`, an optional `.lesson`) or a plain `.case-cite` line for the citation itself, matching the exact citation format used throughout `principles.html`. Every section after the first opens with a bridging clause connecting it to the section before it, per the numbered-sections failure mode below; sections must read as one connected argument, never as an interchangeable, shuffleable list.
3. **Closing "how to spot it" section**: a `.questions-callout` with 2-4 `<li><b>Question?</b><span>Why it matters.</span></li>` items, the same component `nobody-owns-a-bias.html` and `six-moves-behind-every-pricing-page.html` already use.
4. **Continue reading**: a final `.report-section` with 2-4 `.method-grid` pairs of `.method-col` links, cross-linking to the principles, other reports, and Science Behind entries the report is built on.

## Failure mode: numbered sections that never acknowledge each other

**What happened.** `six-moves-behind-every-pricing-page.html` shipped with six `.report-section`s, one per pricing mechanism, each opening straight into its own explanation with no reference to the section before or after it. Four of the six read as genuinely interchangeable: shuffle Move 3 and Move 5 and nothing about the piece would feel wrong, because neither one is actually building on what came before. This was caught during a wider audit triggered by the owner's catch on a different content type (a Science Behind entry: "This is missing a narrative that frames up at the start and links each part together. Looks like a set of discreet vinettes.") that turned out to apply here too. A spot-check of several other reports (`every-default-decides-who-pays.html`, `not-testing-is-still-a-bet.html`) found the opposite already true in most of them: `every-default-decides-who-pays.html`'s five sections each open by naming what the previous one established ("The target-date fund above is 'smart' in a specific, technical sense...") before introducing what's new. That's the bar every report's section sequence needs to clear, not just the ones with an obvious numbered list.

**The check.** Cover every `<h3>` in the report body and read only the first sentence of each `.report-section`'s first `<p class="intro">` in order. Does each one reference, contrast with, or build on the section before it, or could the sections be shuffled into any order with no loss of sense? A report can have a strong `.story-stack` thesis up top and still fail this check in the body: the thesis states the argument's conclusion, it doesn't make each body section aware the others exist.

**The fix, in practice.** Every `.report-section` after the first opens its first `<p class="intro">` with a bridging clause naming what the prior section covered and why this one is the next thing that matters, exactly the discipline `every-default-decides-who-pays.html` already models. A numbered list ("Move 1," "Case one") is not itself a bridge, the numbers imply order without ever stating the relationship between adjacent items, which is exactly how `six-moves-behind-every-pricing-page.html` shipped with numbers but no real connective tissue. The final section (usually the last mechanism or case, before the closing "how to spot it" callout) should close by explicitly naming the pattern across every section, not just its own, so the report has a real ending instead of just running out of items. This costs nothing structurally, it's a sentence prepended to an existing `<p class="intro">`, not a new component.

## The chunking rule: a long intro paragraph reads as a wall of text on mobile

**What happened.** Every prior report's `.toc-intro` ran 4-5 sentences, roughly 90-110 words, one paragraph. `not-testing-is-still-a-bet.html`'s intro ran 9 sentences, about 180 words, still one paragraph, because it needed to set up four real cases instead of one or two. On a real phone screenshot it reads as a single dense block above the fold, distinct from every report before it. The owner's catch, verbatim: "Chunk this content. It's too much a block of text. But keep elegant."

**The check.** `.toc-intro` has no hard character limit in CSS (`max-width: 46ch`, so it wraps narrow regardless of length), which is exactly why a long one silently becomes a wall of text instead of throwing an error. Before finalizing, count the intro's rough word count. Past about 110-120 words or five sentences, look for the natural idea boundaries: usually the claim itself, then the stakes or scale of the claim, then a roadmap of what follows. That's rarely more than three beats even for a long intro.

**The fix, in practice.** Split into 2-3 separate `<p class="toc-intro">` elements, one per idea, no bolded *lead-in labels* and no restructuring of the sentences themselves beyond the split. `.toc-intro`'s existing `margin: 0 0 16px` already spaces stacked paragraphs correctly, so this is a pure content split, not a CSS change. This is deliberately lighter-touch than `experiment-blueprint`'s bolded-lead-in chunking rule (`<b>The study.</b>`, `<b>The gap.</b>`): a report's intro is the site's own editorial voice making an argument, and a checklist-style label opening every paragraph would flatten that into a spec sheet instead of a pitch. Keep the labels for technical blocks; keep report intros as plain, short paragraphs opening in the site's own voice, matching the rest of the site's op-ed register. The same instinct applies inside `.report-section` bodies: if a single `<p class="intro">` is doing more than one job (stating a finding, then immediately pivoting to its caveat, then to its implication), that's usually two paragraphs wearing one tag.

**"No bolded lead-in labels" is not the same rule as "no bold at all," and a split paragraph with no bold anywhere in it still reads as a slab.** `proximity-gets-mistaken-for-merit.html` shipped its first draft with the chunking rule above correctly applied, `.toc-intro` and `.report-section .intro` paragraphs split at natural idea boundaries, and still drew, on the very next round: "Same chunking. You're not solving. Just slab of text. Highlight key small parts with bold." A paragraph of even 60-90 words with zero visual variation inside it gives a scanning eye nothing to land on. Every `.toc-intro` and `.report-section .intro` paragraph needs 1-2 short `<b>`-wrapped phrases, mid-sentence, bolding the actual finding, the number, or the sharpest clause, the same inline-emphasis pattern `reading-the-research.html`'s own `<b>internal validity</b>` / `<b>external validity</b>` already uses. This is a different device from a lead-in label: a label sits at the front of a paragraph and announces its category ("The study."); an inline bold sits wherever the load-bearing phrase actually falls in the sentence and doesn't turn the paragraph into a checklist. Do both checks (word count *and* presence of inline bold) before calling any intro or report-section paragraph finished, not just the word-count check.

**This exact rule was already written down here after `proximity-gets-mistaken-for-merit.html` got caught, and it still didn't stop the next report from shipping with the identical defect.** On 2026-08-26 an audit of every `.toc-intro` and `.report-section .intro` paragraph across all 14 special reports found real, unmissable inline bold in exactly one file, the one this rule was originally written for. The other 13, including `the-tactile-premium.html`, a report written *after* this section already existed in this skill, shipped with zero `<b>` tags across roughly 100 paragraphs between them. The owner's catch, verbatim, on `the-tactile-premium.html`: "Same issues I've pointed out MANY times... slabs of content but need to chunk and highlight key parts with bold... go back to review against all current content and apply to all future content." Writing a rule down once is not the same as it being load-bearing. **Before calling any special report finished, actually grep it**: `grep -c '<b>' file.html` inside every `.toc-intro` and `.report-section .intro` block, confirm the count is at least one per paragraph over ~15 words, not just recall that this rule exists. This is a mechanical, five-second check with zero excuse to skip it, and skipping it is exactly what happened here.

## Evidence-heavy reports: chunk a summary above the detail

**What happened.** `breaking-a-form-into-steps.html` built up six academic sources across five `.report-section` blocks (a pattern-vs-pattern head-to-head, a task-length qualifier, a source-fidelity ranking), each with its own study-cards and paraphrase tags. The owner's catch, verbatim: "Need a more succinct summary for the evidence with chunked and iconography above the evidence details." The dense, correctly-sourced version was still the wrong thing to put in front of a reader before they'd decided whether to read it at all.

**The fix, in practice.** A `<p class="story-stack-label">The evidence at a glance</p>` plus a `.story-stack` of 3-5 `.story-stack-item`s (bespoke icon + `<h4>` + one-sentence `<p>` with a real number in it), placed at the very top of the report body, before the first detailed `.report-section`. This reuses the exact component the header's own "story in four parts" already uses, just populated with the evidence's own headline facts instead of the narrative beats, and titled differently so the two don't read as the same list repeated. Build this any time a report's evidence section runs to more than two or three cited studies: a reader should get the succinct version before the citations, not only after them.

## A two-pattern report needs a real comparison visual, not just a comparison in prose

**What happened.** `breaking-a-form-into-steps.html` names two competing UX patterns (Progress Flow, Progressive Load) in its own title and spends the whole report arguing about which performs better. The first drafts explained the distinction entirely in prose. The owner's catch, verbatim: "You didn't apply the visual explanation of thee two forms types. Add this." A report whose entire thesis rests on a named comparison needs to let the reader see the comparison, not just read about it.

**The fix, in practice.** Run this through `oscarfinch-feedback-html` first: build 2-3 real, rendered visual options (not descriptions) illustrating the comparison, let the owner pick and iterate, then hard-code the chosen visual into the report itself, in or right after the `.toc-intro` paragraphs, and register it in `VISUAL-SYSTEMS.md` as its own numbered system (see system #19, `.pattern-compare`, for the worked example). A round of "needs work" feedback on the review artifact caught a real content error before it shipped, not just a cosmetic one: the first version implied "one field per step," when the report's own text defines Progress Flow as "one *set* of fields per step." Check a comparison visual against the report's own stated definitions, not just against how the pattern is commonly drawn elsewhere, before treating "needs work" feedback as purely visual.

## Narrow a well-sourced claim with new evidence; don't edit it

**What happened.** After `breaking-a-form-into-steps.html` shipped its device-reversal finding (Progressive Load beat Progress Flow on a phone, sourced to Mavletova & Couper's 268-question survey), new research surfaced showing the same reversal didn't hold on a short mobile form (Einfeldt & Degbelo). The specific, verified Mavletova claim was still true and still well-sourced; it just wasn't the whole story.

**The fix, in practice.** Leave the original sourced sentence alone and add a new section after it that supplies the missing scope, closing with an `.also-note` that says explicitly what changed and what didn't: "**None of this overturns Mavletova and Couper's phone result above.** It narrows what that result is actually evidence for." Editing the original claim to hedge it would misrepresent what that specific study actually found; a new section that adds the boundary condition is the honest fix. This is the same discipline `the-nuance` sections already apply to psychological mechanisms; apply it to a pattern-performance finding too when new research narrows its scope.

## Disambiguate a coined term from a real term it could be confused with

**What happened.** This report coined "Progressive Load" for one of its two patterns. A genuinely different, already-established UX term, "progressive loading" (a page-performance technique: skeleton screens, deferred content), sounds close enough to be mistaken for it by a reader who's encountered that other term elsewhere. Nothing in the report said so until it was flagged directly.

**The fix, in practice.** Any time a report coins or repurposes a term, search for whether a different real concept already uses a near-identical name, and if one exists, add a short paragraph naming both the near-miss term and how it differs, right where the report's own term is first defined. This is a variant of the site's own "verified vs. paraphrased" honesty discipline: telling the reader what a term does *not* mean is as much a citation-accuracy issue as getting a number right.

## Verify a citation the user hands you exactly as hard as one you find yourself

**What happened.** The owner pasted in an external research write-up (sourced from another tool) with specific citations, sample sizes, and a stated preference ratio ("6 of 7 preferred the progressive form") to fold into this report. Independent verification found the real ratio was 5 of 7, and found that one other cited study's most specific claim (an asymmetric result on which exact metrics differed) couldn't be corroborated between two secondary sources and had to be dropped rather than stated as fact.

**The fix, in practice.** A citation arriving from the user, in a polished write-up with real-looking numbers and a full reference list, gets the identical treatment as a citation found via this site's own research: verify title, authors, sample size, and the specific number being quoted independently before it goes in, correct anything that doesn't check out, and drop or caveat anything that can't be confirmed rather than trusting the source's own confidence. Well-formatted and specific is not the same as verified.

## Voice and rigor, same as everywhere else on the site

- **No em dashes**, anywhere. See `CLAUDE.md`'s standing policy.
- **Run the `authentic-voice` checks** before calling any report finished, headings included, not just body prose.
- **Never fabricate a citation, a number, or a URL.** A narrative source (a podcast, a public talk) is fine to use for the story, but label it separately from the academic paper it's built on, and verify both are real before publishing.
- **Sync cross-links both ways.** A new report needs a nav-sublist entry (all pages), a `reading-the-research.html` case-card, the "Show N related essays" count, and the homepage "N special reports" count, all updated together, not just the new file on its own.
- **Add it to `search-index.js`, and check its own defining word is actually in the blurb.** A report's own slug, nav tagline, or the exact phrase it's built around (like this report's own "It costs more to print" not containing the word "tactile") needs to actually appear in the indexed title/blurb text, not just be implied by a paraphrase, or the one term a reader most plausibly searches for returns nothing. See `predictive-search-component`'s "Entry-content policy" for the full failure mode and the check to run.
