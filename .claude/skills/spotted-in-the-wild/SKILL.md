# Spotted in the Wild

## Why this exists

Founded 2026-08-26 on a direct request: a screenshot of GoDaddy's domain checkout, showing a "CHOSEN BY OVER 225,000 CUSTOMERS EACH MONTH" badge on an upsell, to be worked into the Social Proof principle article as a real, current example of the mechanism in the wild. The user's own framing: "This will become a skill we develop for each screenshot for behavioural articles uploaded and special reports etc." Every future screenshot should go through the same crop → locate → highlight-review → integrate pipeline, not a one-off process invented per image.

## What this content type is, and isn't

It's a **real, third-party UI screenshot** offered as a live instance of a mechanism already established by the article's own academic citation. It is not the evidence itself, the cited study (Salganik et al. for Social Proof, or whichever paper the target article already cites) is what proves the mechanism is real; the screenshot only shows a real company deploying it today. Never let a screenshot's caption imply it demonstrates the effect worked, a vendor's own "225,000 customers" badge is an unverified marketing claim, not a controlled measurement, and the honesty standard this site holds academic citations to (verified vs. paraphrased, sample size, method) applies here too: **a screenshot proves a tactic is in use, never that the tactic caused anything.** This is also distinct from `field-session` (personal photos of the owner's own talks and work) and `principle-mechanism-diagram` (an invented SVG diagram illustrating the cited study's own finding, not a real product).

## The pipeline, in order

1. **Crop out true chrome and any personal information, but keep real page context in.** True chrome is OS/browser furniture that carries no information about the product (an address bar, a share-sheet toolbar, a tab switcher): always safe to remove. Personal information (a name, address, account number, order ID tied to a real person) must always be removed, no exception, regardless of how much context it would otherwise add. Everything else, product name, price, what step of the flow this is, the button the user is about to press, is real context a reader needs to judge how the tactic sits inside an actual purchase, and defaulting to stripping it out for a "cleaner" image is a mistake this skill made on its own first real case: two screenshots were cropped down to just the isolated badge and rating line, and the result read as generic UI fragments with no way to tell they were real checkout screens at all. The user's own catch, verbatim: "you've taken too much of the screenshot away so context can't be seen." The fix was to re-crop from the original source with a far more generous frame, the full product/price header through to the actual payment button in one continuous, unedited region (skipping only true chrome and, on the personal-information screen, skipping the name/address block entirely by starting the crop below it rather than trying to redact and splice), then draw the same pixel-located highlight inside that bigger frame. When in doubt about how much surrounding UI to keep, keep more, not less; a highlighted claim floating in an otherwise-empty crop is what triggers this failure, not a highlighted claim sitting inside a recognizable, real screen. Check the final crop for any watermark or platform overlay per `CLAUDE.md`'s standing no-watermark policy either way.
2. **Locate the exact claim by pixel boundary, not by eye.** Before drawing any highlight, find the real pixel bounding box of the specific text or number being cited by scanning actual RGB values for the color transition between the claim's background (a badge, a highlighted row) and the surrounding page background, not by eyeballing coordinates off a scaled-down preview. A screenshot rendered to the model is not pixel-accurate to its saved file dimensions; sample real pixel colors in the actual saved file (Python + Pillow, scanning rows/columns for a color transition) to get an exact box, then pad it by roughly 8-10px for the highlight.
3. **Build five highlight-style options and let the user pick.** Default set, proven on the first real case, reuse these five unless the specific screenshot calls for something else:
   - **Clean outline** — a rounded-rect stroke around the claim, in the site's terracotta.
   - **Highlighter marker** — a semi-transparent mustard rectangle behind the text, mimicking a real highlighter pass.
   - **Spotlight, dim the rest** — everything outside the claim dimmed (ink at roughly 70-75% opacity), the claim itself left at full brightness with a thin outline. The most dramatic option; best when the claim is the entire reason the screenshot is being shown.
   - **Hand-drawn circle** — 2-3 slightly offset overlapping ellipses in terracotta, faking a sketchy hand annotation rather than a clean UI callout.
   - **Arrow + caption chip** — a small pill restating the number/claim in the site's own type, with an arrow pointing at the real text. Only works if there's genuine empty space near the claim to place the chip without covering other text; check this before committing to the layout, the first pass of this option covered the bullet list underneath and had to be repositioned into empty space beside the price column instead.
   Render all five as real images (not descriptions) inside this project's standard `oscarfinch-feedback-html` options-review template (binary toggle + comment per option, one copy-feedback button), per this project's own standing policy that any "options in HTML" request uses that template automatically.
4. **Wait for the pick, and expect a hybrid.** The first real case wasn't a straight pick of one of the five, it was "clean outline, use this, maybe add an elegant red arrow, not intrusive": the outline treatment plus a small, thin, single-color arrow (site's terracotta, ~3px stroke, short, no chip or label attached) pointing at the box from nearby empty space. Treat the five as a starting palette to combine, not five mutually exclusive final states. Route a request like this back through the same pixel-boundary discipline: find real clear space near the box (verify it's actually background-color, not text, by sampling pixels) before placing the arrow, and keep the arrow short and thin enough that it reads as a pointer, not a second highlight competing with the outline.
5. **Finalize and save.** Save the chosen image to `mockup/images/` with a descriptive filename following the site's existing convention (`source-context-slug.jpg` or `.png`, lowercase, hyphenated, e.g. `godaddy-domain-protection-social-proof.png`), not a generic name.
6. **Integrate as a new "Spotted in the wild" block** inside the target article's `article-body`, positioned after the existing `.article-block`s (psychology / errors / help) and before the `.study-card`, so the reader meets the mechanism explanation and the real citation first, then sees it deployed live, then reaches the academic proof. Use the `.spotted-wild` CSS component (`styles.css`): a bordered card matching `.study-card`'s tokens, holding one shared intro sentence, a `.spotted-grid` (2 columns on desktop, 1 on mobile via a 640px breakpoint) of `.spotted-item`s when there are multiple screenshots from the same real flow, each with its own `.spotted-caption`, and one shared closing `.spotted-disclosure` at the end rather than repeating the honesty caveat per image, when every image in the block comes from the same source and date. Caption format, every time: what the real product/company is, the exact quoted claim, the date captured. The vendor's-own-unverified-claim caveat can live once in the shared `.spotted-disclosure` for a multi-image block, or per-caption for a single-image block, but it must appear somewhere in the block every time, it's the load-bearing honesty check for this whole content type.

## Two mechanical gotchas found while building the first real block

- **A screenshot rendered inline to the model is not pixel-accurate to its saved file.** Coordinates read off the displayed image will be off; always reload the actual saved file and scan real pixel values before computing any box or clear-space check, per step 2 above. This bit twice: once finding the badge's true boundary, once discovering a "clear" area next to a badge was actually still inside the card's off-white background rather than the page's slightly different off-white, requiring a second, finer pixel scan to find the real transition point.
- **A tightly-cropped screenshot (little margin around the content) breaks the "spotlight" and "hand-drawn circle" options and needs a wider canvas for anything drawn outside the existing frame.** On the second screenshot in the first real case (a two-line ratings badge filling almost the entire crop), spotlight lost its dramatic effect (nothing left to dim), and the circle's sketchy wobble grazed the surrounding text at the edges because there wasn't enough real margin to wobble into. When a highlight needs to extend beyond the tightest reasonable crop (an arrow with clearance, a circle with real breathing room), re-crop from the *original* source image with extra padding on the relevant side rather than padding the already-cropped file, and when programmatically cropping a working image to trim empty space back down afterward, clamp the crop bounds to the image's actual dimensions, `Image.crop()` silently pads out-of-bounds regions with solid black instead of erroring, which shipped once as an unexplained black bar at the bottom of a finished image before it was caught by re-viewing the file.
- **When personal information sits between two regions you want to keep, don't redact or splice, just start the crop after it.** A real order-summary screenshot had a name and home address between the header and the rest of the page worth keeping. Compositing two non-adjacent regions together to skip the middle leaves a visible seam and looks edited, which undermines the whole point of showing something authentic. Instead, find the exact pixel row where the personal-information block ends (the same pixel-scan technique as locating a highlight box) and crop the single continuous region starting a safe margin below that row. The result is still one unedited, contiguous real screenshot, just missing its very top portion, which reads as an honest crop, not a doctored one.

## Three more, found on the second real case (McDonald's, 2026-08-28)

- **Test the exact rectangle a chip would occupy, not a band near it.** Step 3's arrow-and-chip option was checked against a region starting below the target price, came back pure white, and the chip still landed on top of the word ending the headline's previous line, because the chip is 46px tall and grew upward into text the check never looked at. Compute the chip's real box first (`x, y, x + textwidth + padding, y + height`) and sample that. On this screenshot two of the three targets had verified clear space and the third had none anywhere nearby, so the option shipped with two chips instead of three rather than forcing a third into occupied pixels. **Fewer annotations is the right answer when the space isn't there.**
- **A full-height phone screenshot is unreadable at `.spotted-grid`'s default half-column, and the fix is partly a crop and partly CSS.** A 1179x2304 screenshot rendered about 250px wide on desktop, putting the cited "$3.00" at roughly six pixels tall: the evidence stopped being legible, which defeats the block. Two changes fixed it. `.spotted-grid--single` (in `styles.css`) gives a lone item the whole column, because half of it is right for a magazine cover and wrong for a phone. And the crop ends below the last row that carries a highlight rather than at the bottom of the screen. That second one trades away some context, so it needs a real justification each time: here it kept the app header, the whole branded offer card, the checkout line and both product rows with prices, so it still unmistakably reads as a real purchase screen, and it is still one continuous unedited region. **The "keep more context" rule is about staying recognisable as a real screen, not about keeping every pixel.** When cropping to a card boundary, find the card's actual bottom border by pixel scan and clear it: a first pass ended 38px short and sliced the card's border off mid-stroke.
- **Highlight the comparison the screen already contains, not just the one claim.** The interesting thing about this screenshot was not a single number. It was that the same 99 cent price is written two ways on one screen (`99¢` with no dollar sign, `$0.99` leading with a zero), and that the same item's ordinary price further down is `$3.00`, round and un-gamed. Three boxes, one treatment, and the caption naming what each one is doing. Where a real screen puts the mechanism and its own counter-example side by side, box both: it is stronger evidence than the claim alone and it costs nothing extra.

## Voice and rigor, same as everywhere else on the site

- **Never let the caption blur "a real company uses this tactic" into "this tactic worked here."** The cited academic study is the site's evidence; the screenshot is an example of deployment, always phrased that way.
- **No em dashes.** See `CLAUDE.md`'s standing policy.
- **No watermarks.** Check every screenshot before it reaches `mockup/images/`, the same standing policy that applies to every other image on the site.
- **Identify the named company if it isn't a household name**, per the identification rule already established in `science-behind-article`. A brand like GoDaddy is recognisable enough to skip this; a smaller or less familiar one needs a one-clause identification before the caption uses its name again.
