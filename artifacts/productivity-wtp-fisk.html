Fisk: “Customers almost never pay for productivity”

Take the Twitter-shaped claim apart before we run behavioural analysis on it. Could not pin the exact tweet from the paraphrase alone, so this fisks the claim as you restated it.

Ask: Mark each section Looks good / Needs work. Next pass is the behavioural analysis (this claim + the AI agency / reactance angle).

Claim as paraphrased

Consumers will almost never pay for productivity features or apps, which is why the ad model is most common. Corporates and businesses will pay.

Verdict

Mostly true as a directional rule, false as an absolute

Mostly holds
The consumer half is directionally right: most people will not pay for generic productivity on their own card. The business half is also right: firms pay, often a lot. The causal leap (“which is why ads are most common”) is the weak joint. Ads dominate where the product is attention-native and free-to-use at scale. Plenty of successful productivity businesses never use ads at all; they just sell to the buyer who is not the end user.

Three claims, three grades

  • Consumers almost never pay for productivity. Soft true. Conversion is low (often 2–5%), not zero. Power users and identity-tied tools do convert.
  • Therefore ads are the most common model. Overstated. Freemium-to-B2B upgrade and pure subscription are the winning productivity models. Ads are common for consumer utilities and social, not for Notion / Slack / Todoist.
  • Businesses will pay. Strong true, with a caveat: they pay for organisational friction removal (admin, security, seats, compliance), not for the same “make me personally faster” pitch that failed on consumers.
Claim 1

Do consumers almost never pay for productivity?

Half true
“Almost never” is too absolute. What the data actually shows is low free-to-paid conversion and a stubborn ceiling on how many people will put productivity on a personal card, even when the product is popular. That is enough to make consumer-only productivity a hard business, without needing the claim that nobody pays.

Evidence that supports the spirit

  • Consumer freemium conversion commonly sits around 2–5%; B2C SaaS medians are often lower still (~1–3%).
  • Notion: ~4% of 100M+ users on paid plans. Todoist-class productivity tools report similar single-digit conversion.
  • Consumer AI, 2026: PNC via a16z, ~2.2% of consumers paying for AI at ~$31/mo average (May). Bank of America: ~3% of US consumers. TechCrunch’s takeaway: labs are pivoting hard to enterprise because consumer ARPU does not cover cost.
  • a16z (Feb 2026) on ChatGPT usage: most queries are personal productivity (email, search, advice). Those uses rarely justify a personal subscription the way coding does. Altman’s own line about bringing AI to “billions who can’t pay for subscriptions” is the business admitting the same thing.

Evidence that punches holes in “almost never”

  • ChatGPT still converts ~4–10% of a huge base into paid. Absolute paying users are tens of millions.
  • Consumers do pay when the tool is identity-tied or continuous-value: Adobe Creative Cloud, Microsoft 365 Personal/Family, iCloud storage, password managers, ChatGPT Plus for heavy users.
  • a16z also argues GenAI raised the consumer price ceiling ($20/mo becoming normal; $200 Pro tiers existing). So “won’t pay” is category- and intensity-dependent, not a law of nature.

Sources: RevenueCat / OpenView freemium benchmarks (via secondary summaries); Notion business-model writeups; TechCrunch “ugly economics of consumer AI” (30 Sep 2026); a16z “Of course they’re putting ads in AI” (Feb 2026).

Claim 2

Is that why the ad model is most common?

Weakest part
Low consumer WTP explains why some products go free-with-ads. It does not explain productivity software as a category. The typical successful productivity path is freemium → team/enterprise seats, not freemium → banner ads. Ads fit attention products (search, social, short video). They fight utility products, where the job is to get in and out fast.

Where ads do follow from low WTP

  • Consumer AI at billion-user ambition: subscription alone cannot fund free access at that scale when inference is expensive. OpenAI testing ads for free/low-cost tiers is the claim coming true in real time.
  • Meta Muse / ad-funded assistants: personalisation stack already exists, so ads are the natural subsidy.
  • Classic Google/Facebook pattern: free consumer utility funded by ads, then B2B sold separately.

Where the causal story fails

  • Notion, Slack, Figma, Todoist, Linear: no ad model. Monetisation is seats, feature gates, collaboration limits, admin/security.
  • Utility apps often have short, task-focused sessions. Ads need attention. Those incentives clash (a calculator interstitial feels absurd; a feed interstitial feels normal).
  • Netflix, Spotify, YouTube Premium show the other fork: ads as the free tier, subscription as the paid escape. That is still not “productivity apps run on ads.”
  • Cleaner rewrite of the claim: consumers rarely fund productivity themselves, so the product either (a) gets subsidised by ads/attention, or (b) gets paid for by an employer. Option (b) is the productivity industry’s main answer. Option (a) is what consumer AI is now attempting because the cost base is unusually high.
Claim 3

Will corporates and businesses pay?

Strong true
Yes, and the mechanism matters for the later behavioural piece. Businesses do not pay because they love productivity features. They pay because the cost sits on a company card, the benefit is measured in team output or risk avoided, and procurement already expects software invoices. What converts is often not “faster writing” but admin, SSO, audit logs, seat management, data retention, compliance.

What the numbers look like

  • B2B freemium conversion typically higher than pure B2C (roughly 5–15% self-serve; sales-assisted higher). Team/org conversion can land in the teens to twenties for strong PLG products.
  • OpenAI enterprise bookings reportedly doubling through mid/late 2026 while consumer pay rates crawl. Same product family, different buyer.
  • Microsoft 365 Copilot attach is still modest (~3.4% of M365 customers in one cited figure), which is a useful brake: businesses will pay, but not automatically, and not for every AI add-on at any price.
  • Anthropic-style lab strategy (enterprise contracts, vertical expansion) is the industry reading the same tea leaves.

The buyer/user split

  • Consumer: user = payer. WTP must clear a personal budget and subscription fatigue.
  • Business: user ≠ payer. The employee feels the productivity gain; finance/IT feels the invoice. That separation is why the same feature that looks “not worth $20/mo to me” looks “cheap at $30/seat” to a team lead.
Rewrite

A version of the claim that survives the fisk

If we keep the insight and drop the overreach, the claim becomes specific enough to analyse behaviourally.

Proposed steelman

  • Individuals rarely open their own wallet for generic productivity gains, especially when a free tier or free substitute is “good enough,” the time saved is hard to feel, and another subscription is painful.
  • Employers will fund those same gains when they reduce organisational cost, risk, or coordination friction, and when payment is an expense line rather than a personal subscription.
  • Ads appear when you insist on serving the non-paying mass (consumer AI at scale, attention products). They are not the default endpoint of every productivity product; B2B monetisation is.

This is the version worth putting under behavioural analysis next, not the absolute “almost never / therefore ads.”

Mechanisms already sitting under this claim

Not the full analysis yet. Just the shortlist so you can kill any that should not make the cut.

On “why consumers won’t pay”

  • Zero Price Effect (already on Principles): free is a category, not a price. A $0 productivity app and a $12/mo one are not adjacent points on a line.
  • Pain of paying / Payment Transparency: a personal subscription is a vivid recurring loss; a company SaaS invoice is not felt by the user who benefits.
  • Mental accounting: “work tools” feel like they belong on the employer’s bill. Entertainment sits in a different mental pot (Netflix yes, Todoist Pro maybe).
  • Present bias + quantifiability failure: the time saved is diffuse and future; the charge is crisp and monthly. Value is hard to feel at the moment of paywall.
  • Reference prices / anchoring: decades of free Google Docs / free Gmail trained a near-zero anchor for personal productivity.

On the AI agency piece (separate but adjacent)

  • Psychological reactance / Not Enough Choice (already on site): automation that removes the freedom to decide can trigger pushback, especially when unexplained.
  • Algorithm aversion (Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey 2015): people abandon algorithms after seeing them err, even when the algorithm still beats humans. Desire for agency is a named driver.
  • Slight control restores use (Dietvorst et al. 2016/2018): even tiny ability to modify an algorithm’s output sharply raises uptake. Pure “bot decides for you” is the hostile design.
  • Explainability / process transparency: opaque recommendations feel like loss of agency even when the outcome is good. Site already has Operational Transparency / Tradeoff Transparency as cousins.
  • Existing Field Notes neighbour: ai-over-trusted-then-under-trusted.html covers automation bias and blame. Your new angle (agency, reactance, opaque recs) is the other side of that coin, not a duplicate.
