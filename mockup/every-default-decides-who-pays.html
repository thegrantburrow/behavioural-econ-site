    Every default decides who pays for doing nothing

    People talk about “the default” as if one setting covers it: what happens when nobody touches the form. That single label actually packs four design choices. Does a default exist? Which way does it point? When does it take effect? Who was it computed for? Each choice has its own studies behind it, and each one puts the cost of inaction on a different person.

    Those choices decide outcomes that matter. How much of a paycheck lands in retirement savings. Who sits on an organ-donor registry. What an advance directive says about a dying patient's care.

    The sections below walk five real settings of that lever, from a form that refuses to let anyone stay passive to a default built for the person reading it. The country-level organ-donation gap, one of the field's most cited results, also looks different once a newer, cleaner study is on the table. Read that catch before treating the older number as settled.

    The story in four parts

    Opt-out beats opt-in almost everywhere it's tested

    Move a 401(k) or an organ-donor registry from opt-in to opt-out and participation jumps by tens of points, sometimes past 70. The offer itself stays the same.

    One of those results didn't survive a better test

    That famous organ-donation gap came from comparing countries that already differ in a dozen other ways. When researchers tracked five countries before and after a real switch, most of the promised lift vanished.

    Removing the default is its own, different lever

    Strip the default out entirely and force a real choice, and behaviour still moves. Word the decline option around what gets lost by saying no, and it moves further.

    The newest defaults don't wait to be flipped. They're computed per person

    Under a U.S. federal rule, a retirement plan can now key its default fund to the saver's birth year. The setting is built for that person, rather than guessed once for everyone in the plan.

    Four separate decisions hiding inside one word

    Most people who say “the default effect” mean the first two of those four decisions: whether a default exists, and which direction it points. Stretch those settings into a line and they run from a form that refuses to let anyone stay passive, to a default nobody has looked at again in years.

    Decided by youDecided for you
    Active choiceNo default exists
    Opt-inInaction keeps you out
    Opt-outInaction keeps you in
    Persistent defaultNever revisited

    Almost every famous default-effect finding sits in the middle two settings, covered next. The ends of the line get less airtime, and repay closer reading. One end takes the lever away entirely. The other end matters because a persistent default usually stays invisible until someone checks how long it has sat unexamined.

    Opt-in versus opt-out, the effect that made defaults famous

    Eric Johnson, Steven Bellman and Gerald Lohse changed nothing in two online experiments except which way a checkbox pointed. With the box opted-in, further-contact agreement hit 48.2%. Same offer, same wording, opted-out by default: 96.3% agreement. A third arm with no default, forced to tick either way, landed near 70%. So the default was doing more than echoing the choice people would have made on their own. It moved the decision. Default Effect covers the psychology: implied endorsement, the effort of fighting whatever is pre-selected, and a mild aversion to giving up the option currently held.

    Retirement savings

    37% to 86%, identical options, opposite default

    Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea followed one employer's 401(k) before and after new hires moved from opt-in to automatic enrolment. First-six-month participation went from roughly 37% to roughly 86%. Investment options stayed identical. Only the consequence of doing nothing flipped.

    Organ donation

    A 60-plus point gap between two neighbouring countries

    Germany makes organ donation an explicit opt-in. Austria treats consent as presumed unless a citizen opts out. About 12% of Germans are registered donors; Austria's figure approaches 99%. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein's 2003 cross-country comparison put that gap on the map.

    The catch: flipping a default doesn't create behaviour from nothing

    A gap that large, between neighbours that share a border, a language family, and a healthcare system, looks like the default doing all the work. It also matches the trap this site's own Not Testing Is Still a Bet describes: a comparison carrying every other difference between Germany and Austria along with the default. Transplant infrastructure, family-consent practices, public trust in the medical system: none of it held constant.

    Martina Dallacker and colleagues finally separated those confounds from the default itself, the only clean way available: track countries that actually switched. They followed deceased-donor rates in Argentina, Chile, Sweden, Uruguay and Wales before and after each moved from opt-in to opt-out. Averaged across all five, the switch produced no discernible change in the rate of new donors. Living-donor rates, meanwhile, fell significantly after the same switch, a real cost the old cross-country comparison had no way to show.

    A parallel pattern shows up somewhere else entirely. Raj Chetty and colleagues studied Danish retirement accounts in two versions: a subsidy that required an active decision to open the account, and an automatic employer contribution that required none. Each dollar spent on the subsidy raised total household saving by about one cent, because most people who claimed it simply moved money already accumulating elsewhere. The automatic employer contribution, deducted before it ever arrived as spendable income, produced real new saving.

    Those two findings answer different questions. Forcing an active decision can push participation above plain opt-in, because opt-in still leaves the first form to procrastination. Raising the participation rate and creating new behaviour are separate outcomes. An active decision is easy to satisfy on paper and then offset elsewhere. A payroll deduction someone never held in hand is harder to undo that way. On that second question, whether the behaviour is genuinely new, opt-out beats opt-in. For money fungible enough to reshuffle, it also beats a merely forced choice.

    Removing the default entirely: forced choice, and its sharper cousin

    Inertia has nowhere to sit when the design never installs a default. Gabriel Carroll, James Choi, David Laibson, Brigitte Madrian and Andrew Metrick found that mandating an active decision raised enrolment. New hires had to tick a real box before onboarding paperwork would let them proceed. That raised initial 401(k) enrolment by 28 percentage points over a standard opt-in process. Nobody was defaulted into anything. Procrastination simply had no default left to lean on.

    Active choice

    A forced decision, no framing

    Punam Keller, Bryan Harlam, George Loewenstein and Kevin Volpp had employees actively check a box accepting or declining a flu shot, with nothing pre-selected. 62% took the shot, against 42% under a standard opt-in form offered to a comparable group.

    Enhanced active choice

    The same forced decision, framed around the loss

    A third arm in the same study faced the identical forced choice, except the decline option named what it gave up: a higher chance of getting the flu. Agreement rose again, to 75%, and still no default entered the design.

    “Enhanced” does not sneak a default back in. Nothing is pre-selected in either version. The second version borrows the mechanism this site's Loss Aversion article names. A described loss weighs more than an equivalent, unstated gain, even while the choice stays entirely in the decision-maker's hands.

    Two more variables besides existence and direction: when it fires, and whether it's revisited

    An opt-out default need not fire the moment someone signs up. Smart Defaults is this site's write-up of Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow plan. It covers what happens when the same lever is timed against a raise that has not landed yet, instead of a paycheck someone is already counting on. The result is an increase in savings that never has to feel like a cut, because there is nothing yet to feel a cut from.

    Whether a default ever gets revisited once set is a separate failure mode, and it can compound while the file stays closed. Default Effect covers Brot-Goldberg and colleagues' study of Medicare Part D beneficiaries randomly assigned a starting drug plan. Years later, most were still on it regardless of how badly it fit their prescriptions. That persistence tracked passivity more than satisfaction.

    One design answers that failure without asking the saver to act again. Since the Pension Protection Act of 2006, U.S. retirement plans have been allowed to set a target-date fund, keyed to a saver's expected retirement year, as the default investment for anyone who never actively chooses one. The fund keeps shifting its own mix from riskier to more conservative holdings as that date approaches, on a schedule fixed the day the saver was defaulted in. So the default keeps adjusting toward something like the saver's real situation without a second decision from them.

    Personalised defaults, and a second use of the phrase “smart default”

    The target-date fund above is “smart” in a technical sense that has nothing to do with timing. N. Craig Smith, Daniel Goldstein and Eric Johnson use the same two words, smart defaults, for something different from this site's Smart Defaults principle. Here the idea is a default computed from data about the specific person receiving it, rather than copied from whatever setting works best for the average person in the pool. A target-date fund keyed to birth year is a mild version: one input, age, personalises an otherwise identical default across an entire workforce.

    Smith, Goldstein and Johnson's account of why any default carries this much force names three causes. It reads as an implicit recommendation from whoever set it. It exploits the same cognitive shortcuts and effort-avoidance covered in Default Effect. And it removes a real cost, the time and attention choosing would take, that a forced choice still demands. Craig McKenzie, Michael Liersch and Stacey Finkelstein's experiments on that first cause found people really do read a policy default as advice. Told that a retirement plan's default contribution rate had been set high, participants inferred the plan's designers thought a high rate was the right one, and shifted their own stated preference toward it. Personalising a default does not erase that inference. A default visibly built around one person looks less like an administrative shortcut and more like advice aimed at them by whoever built the model that guessed it.

    Four questions worth asking about any default you're handed

    1. Is there actually a default here, or does the form force a real choice?When neither option is pre-selected, inertia has nowhere to rest, and every agreement or refusal reflects an active decision.
    2. Which direction does doing nothing point?Towards the option (opt-out) or away from it (opt-in) decides whether the people who end up enrolled mostly wanted it, or mostly never got around to leaving.
    3. Is the change timed against something you already have, or something you haven't received yet?A default that only takes effect against a future raise, refund, or renewal costs less to accept than one reaching into money or access you're counting on today.
    4. Has this default ever been revisited, or is it running on whatever guess was made the day you were enrolled?A default that adjusts itself over time, like a target-date fund's glide path, degrades far more slowly than one nobody is ever prompted to reconsider.
