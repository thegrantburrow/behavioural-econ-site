    Every Program Runs on a Theory. Most Never Write It Down.

    Evaluation research has treated program theory as its own craft for decades. A logic model sketches the path from activity to outcome. A program theory states why each join on that path should work. Teams usually draw the sketch. Few put the reasons in writing.

    An earlier report on this site already walked a five-stage funnel with one real metric at each join, built around a savings-goal feature. That piece is a logic model. This one sits one layer down: the causal claims evaluators have formalised for half a century, and the two failure modes that look identical until someone names the theory.

    One of the cleanest cases on record ran almost exactly to plan and still pushed the harm it meant to stop higher. Delivery held. The causal idea did not. Program theory exists so that split can be made on purpose.

    The story in four parts

    A program is a bet on a causal chain

    Every initiative treats doing X as a path to Y, even when nobody ever writes that bet in those words.

    A program can fail for two different reasons

    The idea itself can be false, or a sound idea can be mishandled in the field. Those two breakdowns call for different repairs.

    A logic model draws the chain. Program theory explains it.

    Listing the stages is the lighter job. Naming why each transition should fire, and in which conditions, is the part that can be tested.

    Writing the theory down is what makes it falsifiable

    An unspoken assumption cannot be checked before the budget is spent. A written claim can.

    The evidence at a glance

    A well-delivered program increased the harm it was built to prevent

    Across nine randomised and quasi-randomised trials, a Campbell Collaboration review found “Scared Straight”-style programmes lifted reoffending by 1 to 28% relative to untreated controls.

    506 villages, randomised, before a peso was spent nationally

    Mexico's Progresa put its theory of change on trial in 506 of 6,396 eligible villages before national scale-up, among the earliest large randomised evaluations of a national anti-poverty programme.

    Outcomes come in three tiers

    The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's logic-model guide, already cited on this site, divides outcomes into short-term, medium-term, and long-term tiers, the layered map a single goal line leaves out.

    A logic model draws the chain. Program theory explains why it should hold.

    Evaluators have kept the two pieces apart for a long time. Huey-Tsyh Chen's foundational split divides a program's theory into a normative half (what the programme does, and what it aims to achieve) and a causative half (why those actions should produce the result). Sound evaluation needs the causative half alongside the normative one. Funnell and Rogers draw the same line for practitioners. The logic model is the box-and-arrow sketch. Full program theory adds the causal assumptions and the conditions that have to be true for each arrow to hold.

    That is the gap this site's outcome-logic report walks into without naming it. Its five-stage funnel, with a real metric at every join, is a logic model. Program theory sits underneath. It writes that a customer should move from “sent” to “opened” because attention has to clear a salience threshold, as an explicit claim someone can check.

    Sources: Chen, H. T. (1990). Theory-Driven Evaluations. Sage Publications. Funnell, S. C., & Rogers, P. J. (2011). Purposeful Program Theory: Effective Use of Theories of Change and Logic Models. Jossey-Bass. Both build on the same W.K. Kellogg Foundation (2004) Logic Model Development Guide already cited in Your Roadmap Has a Phase 2.

    Once the theory is written down separately, a second question becomes possible

    Putting the causal chain on paper does more than describe the programme. It opens a diagnosis that stays unavailable when the chain stays implied. Carol Weiss's framing of theory-based evaluation sorts failure into two causes. A theory failure means the underlying causal idea was false. An implementation failure means a sound idea was mishandled in delivery. With no explicit program theory to compare against, an evaluator watching a programme fail cannot tell which cause applies, and the wrong call points the repair at the wrong place.

    Source: Weiss, C. H. (1997). “Theory-Based Evaluation: Past, Present, and Future.” New Directions for Evaluation, 1997(76), 41–55.

    Scared Straight is the sharpest real illustration of a theory failure

    The abstract split is easy to recite. Here is the same split inside a programme tested again and again in the field. “Scared Straight”-style programmes brought juvenile offenders and at-risk youth into adult prisons to hear inmates describe incarceration first-hand, on the causal bet that vivid exposure to consequences would deter future crime.

    Real Case

    The real metric, and what it actually showed

    Why did a program executed almost exactly as designed increase the crime it was built to prevent?

    A Campbell Collaboration systematic review pooled nine randomised and quasi-randomised trials of Scared Straight and similar juvenile awareness programmes across eight U.S. states. Each study tracked subsequent offending as the outcome, leaving attendance and satisfaction aside. Pooled across those trials, reoffending rose 1 to 28% among treated youth relative to untreated controls.

    What it teaches: delivery mostly matched the plan. Prisoners spoke, tours ran, sessions took place. Tighter management would not have rescued the result, because the causal bet underneath it, that fear of a vivid future consequence cuts present offending, failed for this population. Call that a theory failure. An implementation failure looks different, and only a written theory lets anyone tell the two apart after the fact.

    Petrosino, A., Turpin-Petrosino, C., Hollis-Peel, M. E., & Lavenberg, J. G. (2013). “Scared Straight and Other Juvenile Awareness Programs for Preventing Juvenile Delinquency: A Systematic Review.” Campbell Systematic Reviews, 9(1), 1–55

    Progresa is the reverse case: a theory specific enough to be tested

    Scared Straight is what follows when a false theory scales without a prior check. Mexico's Progresa programme ran the opposite sequence. Designed in 1997 by deputy finance minister Santiago Levy, Progresa (later renamed Oportunidades) paid cash transfers to mothers in poor households, conditional on children attending school and family members attending health checkups.

    Real Case

    The real metric, and what it actually showed

    What made Progresa's theory testable instead of just hoped for?

    Designers wrote the theory as a concrete causal chain rather than a soft goal. Cash tied to school attendance and health checkups would raise human-capital investment, and that investment would cut poverty in the next generation beyond short-term relief in the current one. That level of detail is what let evaluators randomise 506 of the 6,396 eligible villages into treatment and control groups before national rollout. It remains one of the earliest large-scale randomised evaluations of a national anti-poverty programme anywhere.

    What it teaches: a sharp theory does not promise success. It promises that success or failure, and the reason for either, can be measured, which is why writing the theory down is worth the time.

    Read together, these two cases teach the same lesson from opposite directions

    Scared Straight and Progresa sit side by side as two tests of the same evaluation habit. Both cases support the same claim about how to evaluate. A named, specific program theory is what let Scared Straight's collapse read as a theory failure instead of an unreadable shrug. The same discipline is what let Progresa's designers put their causal claim on trial before staking a national budget on it. Nobody could see either outcome in advance. Writing the theory down, in enough detail to check, is what made each result readable after the money moved.

    Before you fund a program, or fund it again, four checks

    Here is the same discipline in a form a team can run in a meeting. Four checks, short of a full evaluation plan, because a theory that stays unwritten when the process feels heavy never gets the chance to do its job.

    1. Is the causal chain on paper, beyond the goal line?“Reduce poverty” and “reduce delinquency” name destinations. The chain names the steps expected to get there.
    2. For each link, can the team say why it should hold, and under which conditions?“Kids will be scared into compliance” is a claim. Saying why, and for which kids, is the theory.
    3. If the programme fails, could anyone tell a false theory from broken delivery?With no written theory to compare against, a failed programme reports only that it failed.
    4. Has anyone tried to break the theory on paper before spending money on a field test?Progresa's theory cleared that scrutiny before scale-up. Scared Straight's theory never faced the same paper test.
