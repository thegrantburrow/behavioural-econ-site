Does naming a savings pocket keep the money there?

If “Savings Pocket 2” became “Save for Phone,” would less of it get spent?

Builds on the Airds High School field session · Mental Accounting & Salience

Theory

Mental Accounting says money doesn't sit in one undifferentiated pool in people's heads — it gets sorted into labelled accounts, and a labelled account is protected in a way an unlabelled one isn't. Salience does the actual labelling work: a name you can picture (“Save for Phone”) stays visible to the mind in a way a generic one (“Savings Pocket 2”) doesn't. The Airds field session tested this live, with paper envelopes and thirty teenagers, and watched $30 accumulate in the “save” envelopes that nobody consciously decided to save. This is the same mechanism, run as an actual randomised test inside a live banking app instead of a classroom.

Hypothesis

Prompting a customer to name a new savings pocket at the moment they open it — rather than defaulting to an auto-generated label — will reduce the share of that pocket's balance withdrawn to zero within 90 days, without adding enough friction to meaningfully hurt onboarding completion.

Assumed baseline

Illustrative, not real product data. Assume 30% of customers who open a second savings pocket withdraw its full balance within 90 days — the generically-labelled pocket behaving less like “savings” and more like a second everyday-spend account with extra taps.

Does showing the real cost of “minimum payment” change what people pick?

CBA's own research proved this for choosing a card. Would it hold for paying it off?

Builds on Tradeoff Transparency — the real Buell & Choi (2025) field experiment run inside Commonwealth Bank of Australia's credit card funnel

Theory

Buell and Choi ran a real field experiment inside CBA's credit card acquisition funnel: showing each card's drawbacks as prominently as its perks — a “Good and the Bad” page, instead of benefits-only marketing — left take-up statistically unchanged, but improved which card people picked. Customers who saw the downsides went on to spend 9.9% more, cancel 20.5% less, and miss fewer payments. The full teardown, with the real numbers, is on this site's Tradeoff Transparency page. That experiment tested one moment in the funnel: which card to get. It didn't test the next one — once approved, how to pay it off — the point where a customer sets up autopay and, on most cards, “minimum payment due” sits as the easiest option in the list.

Hypothesis

Showing the real cost — projected payoff time and total interest — next to the “minimum payment” autopay option, at the moment it's selected, will reduce how often customers default into minimum-only autopay, the same way naming a card's drawbacks changed which card got picked — without reducing how many customers activate autopay at all.

Assumed baseline

Illustrative, not disclosed CBA data. Assume 62% of new cardholders who set up autopay choose “minimum payment due” — the path of least resistance among the standard three radio options, and usually the pre-selected default.

Ethical guardrail

This isn't designed to block anyone from choosing minimum payment — some customers need that flexibility, and removing it would be its own kind of harm. The design only makes the real cost visible before the choice, the same restraint the original CBA study used: inform the decision, don't engineer it. See Ethics of Not Experimenting for the fuller case for that line.

Does varying the apology on a second failure save the relationship?

List found the exact same $5 coupon backfired on a repeat failure. Would a different apology hold?

Builds on the Voltage Effect field session's apology-design panel · Halperin, Ho, List & Muir (2022), The Economic Journal

Theory

Halperin, Ho, List and Muir ran this at real scale on 1.5 million Uber riders who'd just had a late ride: a $5 coupon apology paid for itself the first time, restoring future spend. Sent again after a second or third bad ride, the identical coupon backfired — riders spent less than if Uber had said nothing at all. The authors' own explanation: an apology reads as a promise, and reusing the exact same one a second time reads as that promise already broken. The full teardown, with the real numbers, is in the Voltage Effect field session.

Hypothesis

After a first service failure, a costly and specific apology — a fee credit plus a plain explanation of what happened — will outperform a generic scripted apology on post-incident satisfaction. On a second failure within the same window, varying the apology's content and framing will hold that advantage; reusing the identical script, the way most support tooling does by default, will not.

Assumed baseline

Illustrative, not real support-desk data. Assume post-incident CSAT (1–5) currently averages 3.1 after a generic apology message, standard deviation 1.1 — typical for a scripted, one-size response with no real cost attached.

The mechanism under test: not whether to apologise twice, but whether the second apology reads as a new response to a new problem, or the same promise, broken again.

Two-sample means test, equal allocation: n = 2 × (zα/2 + zβ)² × σ² / (μ₁−μ₂)², power 80%. The second-failure comparison is a smaller, non-randomised subset — whether a customer has a repeat incident isn't random — so treat that specific cut as exploratory, not confirmatory, the same caution this site takes with any subgroup result.

Outcome variables
  • ✓Primary: post-incident CSAT (1–5), collected immediately after resolution
  • ✓Secondary (exploratory): CSAT specifically on the second-incident subset
  • ✓Secondary: 90-day retention for affected customers, matching the real spend-based outcome List's team used
  • ✓Guardrail: cost per incident (average credit issued) weighed against retained-revenue estimate — the whole point of List's finding is that this pays for itself
  • ✓Guardrail: support re-contact rate — a good specific explanation should reduce follow-up queries, not just soften the moment
