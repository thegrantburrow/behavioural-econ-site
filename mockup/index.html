Applied behavioural economics

You're already doing behavioural economics.

Probably without knowing it. And probably doing it badly.

This is a field guide to the forces already shaping how people decide — so you can use them deliberately, not by accident.

See it in practice

01

Anchoring

The first number you see becomes the reference point every later number gets judged against — even when it's arbitrary.

Illustration, from the real photo

Photographed on a shop floor: a "Super Deal" sign showing $399.99 next to a struck-through $1,349.99 RRP.

02

Sludge (Obstruction)

Friction added deliberately against your interest — like a cancellation flow that interrupts you with a retention offer instead of letting you leave.

Illustration, from the real photo

A real subscription cancellation flow showing a "before you go" retention offer.

03

Zero Price Effect

Free isn't just a low price — it's a different category of decision. Demand jumps disproportionately the moment a price hits $0 .

Illustration, from the real photo

App push notifications offering FREE items, not discounts.

04

Decoy Effect (Asymmetric Dominance)

Add a third, clearly worse option and people switch to the option it makes look better — even though nothing about that option changed.

Illustration 1
Illustration 2

Two real photos: a subscription pricing screen where "Best Value" is the cheapest option, and a gelato menu's Single/Double/Triple scoop ladder.

05

Operational Transparency

Watching the work happen increases how much people value it — effort becomes visible, and visible effort feels worth more.

Illustration, from the real photo

A fry shop's fully open glass kitchen, "HAND CUT FRIES" signage.
