A field note, not a study

The ethics of not experimenting

Every principle on this site comes with a study, a citation, a methodology critique, because that's the standard the site holds itself to. This one doesn't get that treatment, and it shouldn't pretend to: it's an argument, not a finding, and it's worth saying so plainly before making it.

The argument: choosing not to measure the effect of a change you're making isn't a neutral, cautious default. It's still a bet, just an unmeasured one. Every new feature, policy, or message you ship changes something, whether or not anyone tracks what. “We didn't have time to test it” and “we tested it and it worked” are not equally safe choices; the first is just the second one without the information.

This doesn't mean every decision needs a randomised trial before it ships. That bar is often genuinely too high, too slow, or too expensive for the size of the decision. But it's worth being honest about what's actually being traded away when a team skips measurement: not caution, but the ability to know, later, whether the change helped, hurt, or did nothing at all.

Treat this as one site's argument, not settled research, and feel free to disagree.

The same avoidance shows up as an organizational habit, not just a decision: Don't Say the E-Word covers why so many teams flinch at calling a test what it is.
