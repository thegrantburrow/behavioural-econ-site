    Behavioural economics vs. behavioural science vs. psychology vs. UX

    Scale versus explanation, three stops

    Classical economics

    Models a rational, utility-maximising actor. Cheap to scale, and wrong in predictable ways about how people actually choose.

    Behavioural economics

    Occupies the middle ground on purpose: bounded rationality with repeatable patterns, still aiming at predictions that scale.

    Psychology

    Accounts for the mechanism behind a single decision. That depth rarely compresses into a rule a product team can ship.

    Behavioural science

    An applied umbrella that folds psychology and behavioural economics, plus sociology and anthropology, into practitioner playbooks.

    UX & product design

    Turns that playbook into one interface and one specific flow.

    Shared research base. Five separate jobs.

    No field owns a bias. In 1974 Tversky and Kahneman published the anchoring effect in a psychology journal. Kahneman later took a Nobel Prize in economics for that body of work. One paper, one finding, claimed from the start by two disciplines.

    That overlap is common, and the five neighbouring fields still do unequal work. Classical economics and psychology sit at opposite poles of a real trade-off between scale and explanation, the framing this site's field session opens with in every talk: Where Behavioural Economics Actually Sits. Behavioural economics holds the deliberate middle.

    Findings then travel two more hops before they land on a screen. Behavioural science packages them into practitioner playbooks; UX and product design wire those playbooks into a specific interface. Down that chain the mechanism stays put. What shifts is who studied it, what label they used, and whether that label makes the trip intact.

    Five fields, from raw finding to shipped interface

    Classical economics

    Starts from a rational actor with stable preferences, full information, and no mental shortcuts. The baseline the other four fields push against.

    Psychology

    Chases the mechanism itself: why the mind does this, with or without a market attached. Outlets include the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Money almost never enters the frame.

    Behavioural economics

    Reads those same mechanisms as departures from a rational-actor model, nearly always with a real choice or a real dollar on the line. Publishes in economics and marketing journals, and carried the original psychology work to its first Nobel.

    Behavioural science

    Youngest of the five: an applied umbrella for government nudge units and corporate behavioural teams. Original research is rare here. The usual job is packaging psychology and economics findings into playbooks and mini-encyclopedias.

    UX & product design

    Puts the same mechanisms into an interface or a price. Sometimes the original research is cited under its own name. Often the finding is rebuilt from scratch under a new label, with no citation at all.

    Beyond psychology: In a January 2022 interview, economist John List said psychology covers only part of behavioural economics. He named sociology, specifically group theory and network theory, as already useful for economic questions, and expected computer science to become the next major contributor. Paraphrased from the interview: the source page sat behind this site's network restrictions, so the wording here is not a verbatim quote. Source: Guy Kawasaki, “John List” (January 2022). A separate point, kept apart from List's remarks: the field session linked above also notes that behavioural economics draws on sociology and anthropology. That matches the National Academies' working definition of the discipline. Source: National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, “Introduction”.

    Anchoring: the label that stays intact

    On this site, anchoring is the cleanest example of a finding that travels through all four applied fields without renaming. Mechanism, label, and a version of the original citation move together, from a 1974 psychology paper into a 2020s ecommerce design guide.

    1974, psychology: “anchoring and adjustment”

    Tversky and Kahneman spun a rigged wheel of fortune, then asked people to estimate what share of UN member states were African countries. The number the wheel stopped on pulled the estimate, even though every participant had watched the spin and knew it was random. Published in Science as one of three heuristics, with representativeness and availability. Full write-up on this site: Anchoring.

    2002: the Nobel goes to economics

    Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this body of work. Tversky had died in 1996, and Nobels are not awarded posthumously. Clearest single fact in the whole overlap: a psychology paper about a rigged wheel is also, officially, foundational economics.

    Applied behavioural science: same label, practitioner packaging

    BehavioralEconomics.com's practitioner-facing bias encyclopedia lists it as “Anchoring (heuristic)”, keeping the original name, with entries drawn from that academic work rather than fresh research of its own.

    UX & product design: same label, product treatment

    Nielsen Norman Group's “The Anchoring Principle” takes the identical mechanism into interface and pricing design. A struck-through “was” price or a pre-selected middle plan does the work the rigged wheel did in 1974, still under the name anchoring.

    Four fields, one continuous paper trail. Later writers did not need a new label because nobody rediscovered the finding alone: each field reached the same original study and cited it.

    Licensing Effect: where the label splits

    Set that continuous trail beside a newer finding: the sense of permission to indulge after doing something responsible. Wherever the effect shows up, the mechanism matches. The labels diverge, because this time each field reached the same result on its own path instead of inheriting one paper trail.

    2001, psychology: “moral credentials”

    Monin and Miller found that people who had first disagreed with a sexist or racist statement later felt freer to express more prejudice, having already shown they were not prejudiced. Published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology under the label "moral credentials." A 2026 registered replication of that specific study found no reliable evidence for it, an honest complication at the finding's own origin point.

    2006, behavioural economics: “licensing effect”

    Khan and Dhar ran an independent consumer-choice version of the same mechanism: imagining a virtuous act tilted people toward an indulgent purchase over a practical one at the same price. Published in the Journal of Marketing Research as the "licensing effect," under its own label rather than Monin and Miller's. Full write-up on this site: Licensing Effect.

    Applied behavioural science: the economics label sticks

    BehavioralEconomics.com's encyclopedia entry is titled “Licensing effect”, Khan and Dhar's economics-side name, even though Monin and Miller published first. The framing that reaches the practitioner audience earliest is usually the one that sticks.

    UX & product design: a third label, no citation

    Consumer marketing writing describes the same allowance as “guilt-free consumption”, softening the guilt a purchase would otherwise carry, with no pointer back to either academic paper. It is a real, separately maintained term, coined by a field that was not hunting for Monin and Miller or Khan and Dhar in the first place.

    Three labels, three independent discoveries, one underlying allowance. The mechanism held together. The paper trail fractured because no single trail existed to inherit.

    Why one label travels and the other breaks apart

    Usefulness barely decides which label survives. Both anchoring and the licensing effect are well-established mechanisms with real, replicated evidence. What mostly decides it is citation order: whether a later field found the earlier paper before inventing its own version. Sometimes the later field ran its own study first, and only afterwards learned someone else had already named the same thing.

    Anchoring had roughly a three-year head start before anyone needed a second label. The 1974 paper already covered both the psychology and the economics of it, because Kahneman worked in both at once. The licensing effect had no shared origin of that kind. Psychology and behavioural economics reached overlapping versions of the same mechanism five years apart, from different questions, in different journals. Neither literature review caught the other in time to share a name.

    Three checks when a “new” finding already feels familiar

    1. Does the citation trail point to one paper, or to several unconnected ones?A shared origin, like anchoring's, usually keeps the label stable wherever the finding shows up. Several separate papers making a similar claim under different labels usually means independent rediscovery of one mechanism, rather than four distinct effects.
    2. Which field's framing reached you first?The name a practitioner encyclopedia, a product blog, or a conference talk uses is usually whichever field's version hit that audience first. Publication date alone does not decide it.
    3. Does the newer label add a real distinction, or only rebrand the same mechanism?Sometimes a field's own name genuinely narrows the claim, the way "licensing effect" points at a purchase choice while Monin and Miller's work covered expressed prejudice more broadly. Other times the new badge is the same finding wearing a different discipline's logo.
