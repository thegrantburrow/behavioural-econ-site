    Proximity Gets Mistaken for Merit

    In 1946, a university let a lottery decide which married student veterans lived next to which. In 2022, a machine-learning study went looking for the same pattern inside a Major League Baseball draft, seventy-six years and an entirely different institution later. Both found it: how close two people happen to live to each other predicts who gets chosen, who gets trusted, and who gets called a good fit, on top of and separate from anything about the person actually being any better.

    The baseball case is the harder one to prove. A university can randomly assign apartments and watch what happens next. Nobody can randomly assign where a scouting director lives, or force a draft to happen twice, once with the real geography and once without it.

    The 2022 study had to make its case a different way, using a real prospect's own statistics and scouting grades to build a stand-in for the random assignment a housing study gets for free. That method, proving a cause when nobody can flip a coin, is common enough across real research that this site now has an entire section built around it: Natural Experiments.

    What follows is both studies read together: the lottery that proved the mechanism, the draft that found it operating on stakes nobody intended, and four questions worth asking of any outcome that gets called merit-based.

    The story in four parts

    A lottery decided who'd become friends, before anyone met

    MIT assigned married student veterans to apartment units essentially at random in 1946, then asked each couple to name their closest friends in the complex.

    Distance beat everything else researchers measured

    Neighbours nineteen feet apart named each other a close friend four times as often as neighbours eighty-nine feet apart on the same hallway, with nothing else about them different.

    Seventy years later, a draft nobody could randomise showed the same pattern

    A 2022 study used machine learning to hold a prospect's real skill constant, and still found scouts favouring the ones who lived nearby.

    The closeness bought worse players, not better ones

    Prospects who benefited from living near their scouting director were 38% less likely to ever appear in a single major-league game.

    A lottery decided who'd become friends, before anyone met

    Leon Festinger, Stanley Schachter and Kurt Back wanted to understand what actually causes friendship, and they found a rare, genuine natural laboratory to test it in. MIT had just built two new apartment complexes, Westgate and Westgate West, to house married student veterans arriving on the GI Bill after World War II. Units were assigned to incoming families essentially at random, before anyone had met a future neighbour or seen a floor plan. Months later, the researchers asked each couple to name their three closest friends within the complex.

    1946, MIT

    Westgate West

    Why did living nineteen feet from someone predict friendship better than anything about who they actually were?

    Because assignment to a unit was effectively a coin flip, any friendship pattern that emerged couldn't be explained by people choosing to live near others they already liked. Whatever caused the pattern had to be built into the building itself.

    The numbers

    Distance did almost all the work

    41% of next-door neighbours, roughly 19 feet apart, named each other a close friend, against 22% of residents two doors down and just 10% of residents at opposite ends of the same hallway, roughly 89 feet away. About 65% of every close friendship named was with someone in the same building, despite equal opportunity to meet people in the complex's other buildings.

    A further detail sharpens the mechanism. Residents whose door sat next to a shared stairwell or mailbox, a spot everyone in the building had to pass, were named as a friend more often than their straight-line distance from others would predict. Raw distance wasn't really the variable. How often two people's actual daily paths crossed was, what Festinger and his co-authors called functional distance.

    Two neighbours don't need to like each other first. They just need to keep running into each other, and the familiarity that builds from those small, unplanned, repeated contacts starts to feel like it was earned through genuine compatibility, not manufactured by a floor plan neither of them chose.

    Festinger, L., Schachter, S., & Back, K. (1950). Social Pressures in Informal Groups: A Study of Human Factors in Housing. Harper & Brothers. The original 1950 monograph isn't available as searchable full text on this site's network, so the figures above are paraphrased from the book's long-standing, widely-replicated numbers as reported across independent academic summaries, not quoted verbatim from the original page.

    A draft can't run a lottery on where a scout lives

    Westgate's evidence is clean specifically because MIT did the one thing no company or league ever does on purpose: it randomised who ended up physically close to whom. That's what makes the finding trustworthy, and it's exactly what a modern institution like Major League Baseball can never offer a researcher. Nobody is going to reshuffle where scouting directors live and re-run the draft to see what changes.

    A 2022 study asked whether the same mechanism, familiarity from repeated exposure standing in for a real judgment of merit, was operating inside the MLB draft anyway. Each team's scouting director evaluates thousands of prospects against a shared pool of statistics, scouting reports and game film, a process built to reward talent regardless of geography.

    Proving that geography was quietly influencing it required a substitute for random assignment: the researchers used machine learning to build a flexible prediction of each prospect's real, draft-worthy skill from everything else on record about him, then checked whether living closer to a team's scouting director still predicted getting picked once that skill estimate was held constant. Read the full breakdown of how that substitute works, and what it can and can't prove, in Natural Experiments.

    2000–2019 MLB Draft

    Roughly 30,000 players, drafted from a pool of over a million

    A player is 7.1% more likely to be drafted for every 1,000km closer he lives to a team's scouting director.

    A separate, smaller effect, about 4.9% more likely per 1,000km closer to the team's home city, held alongside it. Both effects survived holding the machine-learning skill estimate constant, and both were strongest in the draft's later rounds, exactly where a scouting director has the most personal discretion and the least outside scrutiny.

    The cost

    Closeness bought worse players, not better ones

    Players drafted with a proximity advantage were 38% less likely to ever appear in a single MLB game than a similarly-drafted player without it, and appeared in roughly 25 fewer games on average. The bias didn't just reshuffle who got picked when. It measurably lowered the quality of who got picked at all.

    Ahmadi, M., Durst, N., Lachman, J., List, J. A., List, M., List, N., & Vayalinkal, A. (2022). “Nothing Propinks Like Propinquity: Using Machine Learning to Estimate the Effects of Spatial Proximity in the Major League Baseball Draft.” NBER Working Paper No. 30786. Still a working paper rather than a peer-reviewed publication at time of writing. The paper itself sat behind this site's network restrictions, so every number above is paraphrased from its own public summary, not quoted from the full text.

    The mechanism didn't change. The stakes did

    Nobody in the MIT housing complex was doing anything wrong by befriending a next-door neighbour. Nobody in a scouting department is deciding, on purpose, to favour a prospect because he grew up nearby. Both studies point at the same underlying process: repeated, incidental contact builds familiarity, and familiarity gets read as trust, or fit, or knowing someone, well before anyone consciously weighs it as a factor.

    On a hallway, that mechanism produces friendship. Inside a draft, it produces a real player picked over a better one, on stakes a friendship never carries: a roster spot, a signing bonus, a career.

    The site's own write-up of this mechanism, Propinquity Effect, covers the psychology in full, including where the same lever helps rather than hurts: a new hire seated near the colleagues they'll actually need, or a mentorship programme that deliberately builds the same repeated contact a shared mailbox creates by accident.

    Four questions worth asking of any outcome called merit-based

    1. Did the decision-maker have any incidental, repeated contact with the person before the decision?A shared hometown, a shared conference circuit, or even a shared commute route is enough exposure for functional distance to start doing its work.
    2. Is the advantage strongest in the parts of the process with the least oversight?Both studies found the bias concentrated exactly where a single evaluator has the most personal discretion and the least outside review, the draft's later rounds, a hallway's own residents rather than the wider complex.
    3. Did the person who benefited from proximity actually perform better afterwards, or just get chosen more often?The draft study could check this directly, and found the opposite: proximity-advantaged picks were less likely to ever play, not more.
    4. If you swapped the decision-maker's address, would the ranking change?If a plausible answer is yes, geography is doing work a merit-based process isn't supposed to assign to it.
