    How to actually read behavioural science

    A citation on every principle here still leaves a separate job: deciding what that citation can carry. In the 2010s, psychology and behavioural economics ran a hard audit of their own classics. Other labs repeated famous, heavily cited effects with larger samples and locked designs, and many of those effects shrank or disappeared. The field stayed useful. Headline trust stopped being enough.

    Two checks do most of the work. Internal validity asks whether a fair, adequately powered test reproduced the effect in the first place. External validity asks whether it still shows up once people, place, and context change. The cases below cover both: findings that aged poorly under replication, and interventions that aimed one way and moved the opposite way in the field. For how strong the causal claim was before anyone talks about replication, start with How Strong Is That Evidence? A Field Guide to Causal Claims.

    The replication crisis

    Through the 2010s, labs set out to reproduce classic results on purpose. Samples grew. Designs were pre-registered so analysis could not be reshaped after the data arrived. A striking share of famous effects collapsed or shrank under that pressure. How careful readers weigh a claim changed with it. Four cases, and the specific lesson each one carries:

    Replication

    Power Posing

    Can standing like a superhero for two minutes really change your hormones?

    Carney, Cuddy, and Yap's 2010 paper used 42 people. Two minutes in an expansive “power pose” appeared to raise testosterone, cut cortisol, and lift how powerful people said they felt. A TED talk past 20 million views pushed it into mainstream behavioural science lore. Ranehill and colleagues re-ran it in 2015 with 200 participants, enough power to see a real hormonal shift if one existed. Self-reported power came back. Hormones and risk-taking did not.

    What it teaches: Power and polish can outrun the sample that produced them. A tiny original N can print a tidy chart that is mostly noise. Dana Carney, one of the original co-authors, has said publicly that she no longer treats the hormonal and behavioural claims as real.

    Carney, D. R., Cuddy, A. J. C., & Yap, A. J. (2010). “Power Posing: Brief Nonverbal Displays Affect Neuroendocrine Levels and Risk Tolerance.” Psychological Science, 21(10), 1363–1368. Replication: Ranehill, E., et al. (2015). Psychological Science, 26(5), 653–656
    Replication

    Ego Depletion

    Why did 23 labs together find nothing, after decades of studies said willpower runs out?

    Self-control research treated willpower like a muscle for nearly twenty years. Spend it on task A, and task B gets harder because a limited resource is temporarily spent. Dozens of papers and a friendly meta-analysis built a consensus around that story. In 2016, twenty-three labs ran one shared, pre-registered protocol on 2,141 people in total. Pooled across labs, the ego-depletion effect was effectively gone.

    What it teaches: A thick literature can still be thin evidence. Years of small, underpowered studies, plus a file drawer that keeps nulls off the shelf, can manufacture the look of agreement around a weak or missing effect. Pre-registration is there to stop that drift before it hardens into “everyone knows.”

    Hagger, M. S., Chatzisarantis, N. L. D., et al. (2016). “A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect.” Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573
    Replication

    Facial Feedback Hypothesis

    Why did the pen-in-the-teeth effect vanish the moment anyone was watching on video?

    Strack, Martin, and Stepper had people rate cartoons while holding a pen in their teeth (forcing a smile shape) or in their lips (forcing a pout). Cartoons scored funnier in the smile condition. The claim was sharp: the expression itself could shape the emotion people usually treat as its cause. A 2016 multi-lab replication, seventeen labs, more than 2,000 participants, protocol checked with the original author, returned a null.

    What it teaches: A detail nobody flagged can decide whether an effect travels. One leading account: replication labs filmed participants, and being watched appears to damp the very feedback loop under study. A null replication can mean the original was wrong. It can also mean the effect only appears inside a boundary condition that had not been named yet.

    Strack, F., Martin, L. L., & Stepper, S. (1988). Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 54(5), 768–777. Replication: Wagenmakers, E.-J., et al. (2016). Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(6), 917–928
    Replication

    Behavioural Priming

    Why did people only “walk slower” after old-age words when the experimenter expected it?

    Bargh, Chen, and Burrows primed people with old-age words (“Florida,” “wrinkle,” “bingo”) and reported that those people walked more slowly when leaving the lab. The paper shaped a generation of priming work. Doyen and colleagues tried again in 2012 with infrared timing instead of a stopwatch in a human hand. Experiment 1 showed no priming. Experiment 2 recovered a slowdown only when the stopwatch holders had been told, beforehand, to expect slower walking.

    What it teaches: Something real moved the stopwatch reading, and the cause was the wrong one. When the person scoring the outcome knows the hypothesis and is free to judge subjectively (“how slowly did they seem to walk”), expectation can leak into the score. Blinded, objective measurement is the internal-validity guardrail against that leak.

    Bargh, J. A., Chen, M., & Burrows, L. (1996). Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 71(2), 230–244. Replication: Doyen, S., et al. (2012). “Behavioral Priming: It's All in the Mind, but Whose Mind?” PLOS ONE, 7(1), e29081
    Internal, and four more: Five Ways an Experiment Can Be Right and Still Wrong covers this exact failure mode alongside external, ecological, construct, and statistical conclusion validity, the four questions this page's own “internal vs. external” framing leaves for that companion piece.

    Read the four cases as one rule of thumb. A clean-looking single study is still one observation. Before treating any finding on this site, or anywhere else, as settled, check four things: Was the sample large enough to detect a real effect reliably? Was the analysis locked before the results arrived? Has a different lab, with different people, seen the same thing? Does the tested population and setting resemble the place someone wants to apply it? Behavioural science stays worth reading. Methodology has to sit in view beside the headline.

    Lab vs. field: why where you test it matters

    A clean lab demonstration and a result that survives a messy market share a vocabulary word, “effect,” and often little else as evidence. John List has spent much of his career measuring that gap. The honest reading is that it is frequently wider than people budget for.

    Validity

    Lab Generosity Doesn't Always Travel

    Why doesn't how you play a lab game predict how generous you actually are?

    A large share of published generosity and fairness findings come from paid student volunteers who know they are inside a study. Dictator-game giving and punishment of unfair offers are classic examples. Levitt and List reviewed that literature and found lab measures often fail to forecast how the same people act in comparable real situations. Anonymity, social context, and real stakes diverge from the booth.

    What it teaches: Control buys precision and can also rewrite the behaviour under study. Being watched, knowing the session is an experiment, and dealing with strangers who will never reappear are lab features that markets usually strip away.

    Levitt, S. D., & List, J. A. (2007). “What Do Laboratory Experiments Measuring Social Preferences Reveal About the Real World?” Journal of Economic Perspectives, 21(2), 153–174
    Validity

    The Voltage Drop at Scale

    Why do pilots that work beautifully shrink the moment you roll them out for real?

    List's applied programme keeps returning to the same pattern. A strong effect in a small pilot often shrinks, sometimes to a fraction of its size, once the programme runs at real scale. He labels that pattern the “voltage drop.” Fair warning on sourcing: the label and synthesis sit in List's popular book rather than one peer-reviewed paper alone. Treat it as a practitioner pattern from a long field-experiment career, not as a single controlled finding.

    What it teaches: A successful pilot is useful evidence about that pilot. It is not yet evidence about a national rollout. Who gets recruited, who staffs the trial, and the novelty of a small test can all inflate results that fade once delivery becomes ordinary.

    List, J. A. (2022). The Voltage Effect: How to Make Good Ideas Great and Great Ideas Scale. Currency, drawing on List's broader field-experiment research programme, cited throughout in peer-reviewed form.
    The formal name for this: External validity: does it hold up anywhere but here?, on Five Ways an Experiment Can Be Right and Still Wrong, including the 17-lab replication that pinned down exactly why a famous facial-feedback effect only worked when nobody was watching.

    Why asking people what they'll do is unreliable

    Asking “would you pay for this?” or “would you do this?” remains a standard research tool. It measures a different thing from what people do when money or effort is actually on the line, and that gap has a name of its own.

    Validity

    Cheap Talk & Hypothetical Bias

    Why do people say they'd pay more than they actually do, even when warned about it?

    When an answer costs nothing, people reliably report a higher willingness to pay in hypothetical surveys than they hand over once real money is required. Researchers call that gap hypothetical bias. Scripts that warn respondents about the bias beforehand (“cheap talk”) have generally done little reliable work closing it.

    What it teaches: An honest stated intention answers a low-stakes question. It does not forecast the same person's behaviour under a real cost. Wherever the design allows it, an actual purchase or signed commitment beats a stated preference, and this site tries to keep that distinction visible rather than blurred.

    Harrison, G. W., & Rutström, E. E. (2008). “Experimental Evidence on the Existence of Hypothetical Bias in Value Elicitation Methods,” in Handbook of Experimental Economics Results, Vol. 1. Elsevier.
    The formal name for this: Ecological validity: does the task even resemble the real thing?, on Five Ways an Experiment Can Be Right and Still Wrong, including this exact study and this site's own Risk Aversion entry on real versus hypothetical stakes.
