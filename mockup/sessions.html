A session recap, not a study Field Session

Teaching Year 8/9 students the maths their brains skip

Airds High School · Year 8/9 · an “Everyday Banking” talk

When my own kids ask what I actually do at a bank, the answer that works has never been a sentence — it's a demonstration. I got the same chance with a room of Year 8 and 9 students, and used it to run three live demos instead of a lecture, each one built to surface a habit their brains already have, whether they'd noticed it or not.

Anchoring

“How much are you saving?”

I held up a chocolate bar priced at $5, then dropped it to $2.50 — 50% off. “How much are you saving?” Hands went up straight away: “$2.50.” Wrong question. Nobody in that room had $5 in their hand a minute earlier — they weren't saving $2.50, they were spending it. The $5 was never a real price; I'd just planted it as the number everything else would get judged against. It's the same mechanic behind every “was $160, now $80” tag in a shop window.

Why it landed: A wrong answer they'd all just given out loud, corrected on the spot, stuck harder than being handed the definition first.

Mental Accounting

Three envelopes, not one

Three bank envelopes on the table, hand-labelled: Spend for Now, Save for Phone, Save for Future. The rule underneath them is just “a third, a third, a third” — split whatever comes in three ways before deciding what to do with any of it. We simulated ten weeks together, one coin at a time into each envelope, and by the end there was $30 sitting in the “save” envelopes that nobody had consciously decided to save — it just never got the chance to be spent, because it already had a job.

Why it landed: Giving money a label before it reaches your hand protects it from the moment you'd otherwise spend it on autopilot — and keeping it in a separate, less visible envelope does some of that work on its own.

Scarcity & Social Proof

The three spending traps

The last slide put three product tiles side by side, each with one trick highlighted: “4.8★, 1,200+ bought this month” (everyone else already picked it), “flash sale ends in 09:47, only 2 left” (there's no time to think it over), and “50% off, was $160, now $80” (the discount, not the price, does the persuading). None of the three needed to be true to work — that was the point I wanted them to leave with.

Why it landed: Naming the trick out loud, once, made it recognisable later — a few students called out “anchoring!” unprompted at the last tile, having only heard the word twice before.

To a teenager's brain, money isn't a calculator — it's closer to a calculator crossed with Instagram.

What actually stuck wasn't a definition — none of them will remember the word “anchoring” by lunch, and that's fine. What stuck was the moment thirty hands went up for “$2.50 in savings” and got told, on the spot, that they'd just fallen for it. If I ran this again, I'd get real coins into every pair of hands for the envelope demo instead of narrating it from the front — the parts they touched were the parts they argued about afterwards, which is exactly the reaction I was after.

The mechanisms behind this session: Anchoring, Mental Accounting, Salience, Scarcity, and Social Proof — each gets the full study-and-citation treatment on the Principles page. This is what they look like applied to a room of 13- and 14-year-olds instead of a lab.
