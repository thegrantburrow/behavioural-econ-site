    Teaching Year 8/9 students the maths their brains skip

    If a 14-year-old sees “50% off”, why do they treat that as money saved?

    A high school · Year 8/9 · an “Everyday Banking” talk

    When my kids ask what bank work looks like, a live demo beats any job description I've tried. Year 8 and 9 students got the same treatment. I started with sneakers, marked down in front of them, because that demo rarely fails.

    From the actual session: the envelopes, the handout, and the room.

    Live demo, not a controlled study
    A room of Year 8/9 students, no warning about what was coming
    Held up a pair of sneakers. Priced them at $160.
    Dropped the price to $80, live, 50% off.
    Asked the room: “How much are you saving?”
    Hands shot up: “$80”

    They answered the wrong question, which was the lesson. A minute earlier, nobody held $160. Spending $80 is what happened; “saving $80” was the story the old price sold them. That $160 figure was planted so every later number would be judged against it, the same move behind every “was, now” tag in a shop window.

    Why it landed: Correcting a wrong answer they'd just said out loud beat handing them the definition up front. See the full write-up: Anchoring.

    Three saving habits, not a budgeting app

    After the anchoring demo, the room was ready for habits. Envelope rules come next: simple enough to run without a spreadsheet, a calculator, or an adult looking over a shoulder.

    Heuristic

    A third, a third, a third

    Before you decide what any of the money is for, divide it three ways: spend now, save for something concrete, and put the rest aside for later. Skip the tracker and the app. One rule you can keep is enough.

    Mental Accounting

    Name your money

    Label each third before it hits your hand (“spending for now,” “saving for phone,” “saving for future”) and it already feels assigned. Cash without a job is cash your brain treats as free to spend.

    Salience + Friction

    Out of sight, out of mind

    Savings you can't see feel harder to grab, and that friction is why they last. Making a withdrawal slightly annoying is the design, not a bug.

    Why it landed: We ran a ten-week simulation, one coin at a time into each envelope. About $30 ended up in the “save” envelopes without anyone choosing to save that amount. The money already had a job, so it never became spendable. See the full write-up: Mental Accounting, Salience & Good Friction.

    Three spending traps

    Saving habits cover only half the story. The other half is spotting the traps: one slide of the moves shops and apps use daily, whether the scarcity or the social proof is real or not.

    Social Proof

    Everyone has it

    “4.8★ from 860 reviews, 1,200+ bought this month.” Other people's choices bleed into what feels safe, even when a quieter option on the shelf would fit better.

    Scarcity

    Limited time!

    “Flash sale ends in 09:47, only 2 left.” A countdown or low-stock tag pushes a decision before there's time to think, whether the shortage is real or staged.

    Anchoring

    50% off!

    “Was $160, now $80.” The crossed-out figure does the selling more than the $80 does. Same move as the opening demo, now sitting on a product tile.

    Why it landed: Saying each trick once made it spottable later. At the last tile, a few students shouted “anchoring!” without being prompted, after hearing the word only twice. See the full write-up: Scarcity & Social Proof.
    For a teenager, money runs less like a calculator and more like a calculator spliced onto Instagram.

    By lunch the word “anchoring” will be gone, and that is fine. Thirty hands went up for “$80 in savings,” then heard, immediately, that they'd fallen for it. That beat worked because they owned the mistake in public. The envelope bit was weaker: I talked through it from the front instead of putting coins in their hands. Afterwards they argued about whatever they had physically handled. Next time, every pair of hands gets coins for that part.

    The mechanisms behind this session: Anchoring, Mental Accounting, Salience, Scarcity, and Social Proof. Each has the full study-and-citation treatment on the Principles page. Here they show up in a room of 13- and 14-year-olds rather than a lab.
    Taken further: Does naming a savings pocket keep the money there? turns this room's envelope-and-label move into a randomised test inside a real banking app.

    You do not need a talk, envelopes, or marked-down sneakers. Most banking apps already ship the pieces. Here is how those three habits map onto features you likely already have.

    Do this yourself: five minutes
    1 Open your banking app and look for Pockets, Spaces, Goals, or Sub-accounts, most apps now let you split money into named, separate places without opening a new account. If yours doesn't yet, three ordinary savings accounts do the same job.
    2 Pick your split. A third, a third, a third is a starting point, not a rule: the mechanism works at any ratio, as long as it's decided in advance, not after the money's already spent.
    3 Rename each one by its job, not its number. “Save for Phone” beats “Savings Account 2” every time, a name that stands out from the generic ones around it is a name your brain protects.
    4 Automate the split on payday. A standing transfer or an auto-split rule moves the money before you ever see it as spendable, the single biggest lever here, because it removes the moment of decision entirely.
    5 Hide two of the three from your home screen if your app allows it. Keep “Spend for Now” visible, tuck the other two out of the default view. Less visible, less spent.

    Willpower is optional here. Each step locks the decision in before the money hits your hand, so you are not negotiating with yourself after it arrives.

    The mechanisms behind this: Mental Accounting and Salience. Full study-and-citation write-ups sit on the Principles page.

    Finding the “Phase 2” every roadmap skips

    Can you explain, out loud, how your last initiative actually reaches profit?

    Product, Design & Digital teams · a talk on outcome logic and experimentation

    South Park came first. In the underpants episode the gnomes write a plan on a whiteboard: Phase 1, collect underpants. Phase 2, a question mark. Phase 3, profit. Ask about Phase 2 and they freeze. I showed that slide so people could laugh, then pointed out how close it sits to our roadmap decks.

    How the talk opened
    A room of product, design, and digital people, looking at gnomes
    Most of our own initiative slides do the same thing: Customers → Initiative → ? → Profit.
    The “?” gets filled with whatever's easiest to point at: NPS, Engagement, “Project X.”
    None of them are the actual outcome

    Call the gap the Phase 2 Problem. Thomas Sowell put it in a book subtitle: economics is “thinking beyond stage one.” The gnomes stop at stage one. Most roadmap slides do too.

    Why it landed: Asked to defend their own Phase 2 in front of everyone, most people stalled after about one sentence. Same hole the gnomes have, with nicer slides. See the full write-up: Illusion of Explanatory Depth.

    Three reasons the gap is dangerous

    The Phase 2 Problem is funny until you price it. Three costs sit underneath, and none of them mean “stop shipping.” They mean test the cheap link early, before you discover the break at the end of the chain.

    Kohavi et al., 2009

    Most ideas don't work

    On Microsoft's own experimentation platform, roughly one in three tested ideas moved the metric they were meant to move. Ideas that feel obvious still fail most of the time.

    The Voltage Effect

    What works shrinks at scale

    In List's applied work, 50 to 90 percent of programmes that looked strong in a small pilot shed most of that effect once they hit full scale.

    Rube Goldberg

    More links, more that can break

    Each extra step between an initiative and its outcome is another place the chain can snap. Typical roadmaps carry five or six of those links with nobody stress-testing them.

    See also: Lab vs. Field: why where you test it matters, on this site's Reading the Research page.

    The example that landed hardest: “just increase engagement”

    I asked them to map a familiar case: a bank wants more app use. The chain almost writes itself: Customers → Engagement → Loyalty → Retention → Products per Customer → Profit. It looks tidy. Then you check the data.

    Zhang, C. Y., Sussman, A. B., Wang-Ly, N., & Lyu, J. K. (2022). “How Consumers Budget.” Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization, 204, 69–88
    A nationally representative US survey (n=3,826), supplemented with real administrative account data (n=194,678) from a large Australian bank.
    Key findings
    Verbatim (paper text)“We find that engagement is negatively correlated with financial wellbeing (r = -0.34) … engagement decreases with financial wellbeing.”
    ParaphrasedCustomers with the highest digital engagement, the most daily logins, had the lowest financial wellbeing scores in the sample: the opposite direction the naive “engagement equals loyalty” chain assumes.
    Why it landed: Several people had shipped an “increase engagement” initiative before. Seeing the metric they would have celebrated track with financial stress rather than loyalty is when the talk left theory behind.
    Drawing the arrow from Initiative to Profit was easy. Defending it was not.

    Kohavi's number and Sowell's line were scaffolding. The work happened when people mapped Phase 2 for something they had shipped and hit the gnomes' wall themselves. Nobody likes learning their success metric was a stand-in for something else. Order mattered: people who mapped after the engagement twist already knew the punchline, so their maps were softer than the ones who went first. Next time I run the exercise before the reveal.

    The mechanisms behind this session: Illusion of Explanatory Depth. Full study-and-citation treatment sits on the Principles page. Here the room maps its own roadmaps instead of explaining a zipper.

    Five steps from the room exercise. Do it on a whiteboard, not only in your head. Writing forces the gaps into view.

    Do this on your own roadmap
    1 Write down the initiative, and the outcome metric leadership actually cares about, not the metric you're planning to report, the one that pays for the team.
    2 Fill in every step between them, out loud. Not “then it converts”: the actual mechanism, e.g. Trade-offs → Better decisions → Product fit → Retention.
    3 Name a real metric for each step, not a proxy. “Engagement” isn't one; “closure rate %” or “incremental spend $” is.
    4 Ask what could break at each link. This is the Rube Goldberg check: find the weakest join before you build anything on top of it.
    5 Test the weakest link first, not the whole chain at once. That's the one place a small pilot is worth running before scaling anything.

    The map is the deliverable. A chain with one weak link you plan to test beats a blank chain with a confident number taped on the end.

    The mechanisms behind this: Illusion of Explanatory Depth. Full study-and-citation write-up sits on the Principles page.

    Same two-constraint idea as the first session's in-app messages, aimed at teams shipping metrics rather than customers reading them. You need a name that survives a five-minute grilling, and a check short enough to run before a ticket leaves.

    Success-metric review
    • ✓Named the actual outcome, not a proxy, no “engagement,” “NPS,” or “Project X” standing in for profit
    • ✓Mapped every link between the initiative and that outcome, out loud
    • ✓Named a real metric for each link, not a feeling
    • ✓Written down what could break at the weakest link

    Four checks, not forty. A review people will actually run has to survive a tired Friday.

    Why telling customers what's “safe to spend” can make them spend more

    Could the reassuring number on the home screen be driving the overspend?

    Digital, Design & Product teams · a talk on payment friction and spending nudges

    For about a decade, banking-app design has treated more money visibility as an unalloyed good. Show the live balance, show what is “safe to spend” after bills, and people will spend more carefully. I opened with a finding that cuts against that story.

    The finding that opened the talk
    A room of digital, design, and product people, most of whom had shipped a “balance” or “safe to spend” screen
    Buy-now-pay-later purchases get deferred, so today's balance doesn't yet reflect what's already spent.
    That inflates what looks “safe to spend” right now.
    22.2% more likely to make a discretionary purchase

    An overstated available balance gets spent more. Central Bank of Ireland researchers found BNPL raised spending 4.39% versus debit on its own, and the inflated “available balance” left behind did separate damage on top.

    Jose, A., Kelly, J., King, M., & McCarthy, Y. (2025). “Buy Now, Spend More, Pay Later: Behavioural Mechanisms of Buy Now Pay Later Products.” Central Bank of Ireland Research Technical Paper, 15/RT/25
    A field-and-lab experiment on real BNPL and debit spending behaviour, published by Ireland's central bank.
    Key findings
    ParaphrasedBNPL usage led to spending 4.39% more than the identical purchase made on debit.
    ParaphrasedAn inflated perception of remaining balance, left behind by prior BNPL use, made a subsequent discretionary purchase 22.2% more likely, even one with no BNPL option involved.
    ParaphrasedThe mere expectation of future BNPL access raised current debit-card spending by 3.1%, before any deferred payment had even been used.
    See also: When Interventions Backfire on this site's Reading the Research page. Same pattern: a well-meant transparency feature pushing behaviour the wrong way, the way the Energy-Bill Boomerang did for households already using less than their neighbours.

    The gradient underneath it: cash, debit, credit

    BNPL sits on an older curve. Same purchase, same price: the “pain” of paying tracks how the money leaves your hand, cash, card, or credit.

    The baseline

    Cash

    Counting out notes makes the loss vivid and immediate. Cash is the most “painful” way to pay, and it reliably produces the lowest spend.

    Runnemark, Hedman & Xiao, 2015

    Debit

    A till experiment found people willing to pay more on debit than cash for the same purchase. Same money, one step less vivid.

    Prelec & Simester, 2001

    Credit

    In a Boston Celtics ticket auction, credit-card bids ran about 100% higher than cash bids. Same tickets, same room. Only the payment method in hand changed.

    Why it lines up: cash, debit, and credit sit on one continuum of how tightly paying is coupled, in time and attention, to the spend itself. See the full write-up: Pain of Paying.
    When paying hurts less, people buy more. A “safe to spend” figure is credit's old trick dressed as help.

    Most people in the room had shipped a smoother payment flow by stripping friction out. Few had checked whether that friction had been protecting anyone. Nobody plans to bake the credit-card effect into a savings feature, yet that is what an inflated balance does. I audited a stranger's balance screen live, and the pattern still showed. If the room audited its own screens instead of watching mine, they would catch more, and care more.

    The mechanisms behind this session: Pain of Paying and Mental Accounting. Each has the full study-and-citation treatment on the Principles page. Here they show up on a balance screen rather than a coffee purchase.

    Banking apps usually assume more money visibility is always better. A Central Bank of Ireland study cuts the other way. BNPL defers the hit, so today's balance looks too large, and that inflated “safe to spend” figure made a discretionary purchase 22.2% more likely, on top of BNPL itself lifting spend 4.39% over debit.

    Five steps from the room exercise on their own “safe to spend” or balance screens.

    Fix it without losing the feature
    1 Subtract every deferred payment: BNPL, standing orders, upcoming bills, before calling a number “safe.” An inflated figure is the entire mechanism of the backfire.
    2 Show the work, not just the number. A bigger balance offered as pure reassurance removes transparency; showing what's already accounted for restores it.
    3 Borrow from the cash end of the gradient where you actually want to encourage spending less, a brief pause, a running total, a real-terms comparison, not just a bigger number.
    4 Test the actual behaviour, not satisfaction with the new screen. The Central Bank of Ireland finding would be invisible to anyone only measuring whether users liked it.
    5 Re-check every time a new payment method ships. Each new instalment or BNPL option moves the coupling further from cash, and the “safe” number goes wrong in a new way each time.

    Visibility is not the same as honesty. A smaller accurate number protects people better than a larger reassuring one.

    The mechanisms behind this: Pain of Paying and Mental Accounting. Full study-and-citation write-ups sit on the Principles page.

    A checklist that flips the usual product-review instinct. Ask whether you removed the friction that was protecting someone, not only whether you removed enough of it.

    Payment-flow friction review
    • ✓Does the balance shown include every deferred or scheduled payment, not just settled ones?
    • ✓Have we tested whether reducing friction here changed actual spend, not just conversion?
    • ✓Is there a point in this flow where a little vivid friction would protect the customer more than it costs us in conversion?
    • ✓Would we be comfortable defending this number to a regulator as accurate, not just reassuring?

    Four checks, not forty. The last one is the check most teams skip.

    The mechanisms behind this: Pain of Paying and Good Friction, used as a design review rather than a one-off talk.

    Why your best pilot is the one most likely to lose its voltage

    Could staffing a pilot with your very best people be why it fails to scale?

    Product, Design & Digital teams · a talk built around John List's The Voltage Effect

    John List keeps returning to an unglamorous question: why do ideas that look strong in a small pilot collapse once you try them on everyone? The Voltage Effect names that pattern a “voltage drop.” I had already skimmed it in the outcome-logic session. This talk went deeper, using one of List's field experiments and the story that best shows the “very best people” trap product teams walk into.

    Experiment Teardown
    1.5 million real Uber riders who'd just had a ride arrive more than 5 minutes late
    Left with nothing, a bad ride cost Uber future business: riders spent 5–10% less afterwards than riders who hadn't had one.
    Same late ride, same rider, only the apology differed
    Words only
    An apology message: no compensation attached
    + $5 coupon
    The same message, with a $5 credit towards a future ride
    Result Words only + $5 coupon Wins
    Spend Flat → Recovers ✓

    A costly signal repaired the relationship. Words alone did not move it. Send the identical $5-coupon apology after a second or third late ride and it stops working. The coupon that rebuilt trust once backfired on repeat, and riders spent less than if Uber had stayed silent.

    Internal validityA real nationwide dataset: 1.5 million riders' actual future spending, not a survey of stated intent to keep using the app.
    External validityOne company, one type of failure (a late ride). Whether the same backfire threshold holds for a costlier failure, a bounced payment, a frozen card, or a different industry entirely isn't something this study can answer on its own; worth testing before assuming it transfers.
    Halperin, B., Ho, B., List, J. A., & Muir, I. (2022). “Toward an Understanding of the Economics of Apologies: Evidence from a Large-Scale Natural Field Experiment.” The Economic Journal, 132(641), 273–298
    A nationwide field experiment on 1.5 million real Uber riders, run while List was the company's chief economist, not a lab survey.
    Key findings
    ParaphrasedA late ride, left unaddressed, cost Uber roughly 5–10% of that rider's future spend on the platform.
    ParaphrasedA $5 coupon was a more effective apology than words alone, and paid for itself, a positive net return for Uber even after the cost of the coupon.
    ParaphrasedSending the same coupon apology after a second or third bad ride reversed direction, a “backfire effect” the authors attribute to riders reading each apology as a promise, broken again by the next failure.
    See also: The Voltage Drop at Scale on this site's Reading the Research page, and the Voltage Effect trio in the outcome-logic session above (same idea, one study deeper). Government-scale version: Why Governments Need Policy-Based Evidence, Not Just Evidence-Based Policy.

    Three ways a promising pilot lies to you

    The Uber apology finding is strong List research, and it is a different trap: costly signals losing power on repeat. The title of this session points elsewhere. List's “vital signs” are why a room-winning idea dies when it meets everyone else.

    List, Fryer, Levitt & Samek

    Chef, not ingredients

    If the pilot only worked because you put your very best people on it, it will not scale. You cannot clone a star. List's representativeness check asks whether the pilot sample, staff included, looks like the real world or already stacks people who would make anything work. His Chicago Heights preschool experiment (CHECC) used ordinary local teachers on purpose. The result, a 0.22 SD gain in early academic skills across ~600 children, had to survive leaving the pilot.

    Vital sign

    False positives

    A pilot that “worked” can be noise, or novelty that fades once the spotlight is gone. Small samples make luck look like a repeatable effect.

    Vital sign

    Spillovers

    At scale the system around the idea shifts. Uber's rider-growth coupons lifted demand enough to raise fares and wait times, which then killed the demand the coupons had created. A pilot too small to move the market never sees that feedback loop.

    List adds a fifth vital sign this trio has no card for: cost traps. Unit economics from a pilot rarely survive real volume. Support load, manual exceptions, and review time grow faster than the happy path a small pilot enjoys. The “before you scale it” checklist below asks the same thing: do the numbers still work at ten times the volume?

    Citation: List, J. A., Fryer, R. G., Levitt, S. D., & Samek, A., the Chicago Heights Early Childhood Center (CHECC), funded by a $10M Griffin Foundation grant, is documented across several NBER working papers including NBER No. 21477. Reported effect at the 9-month follow-up: +0.22 SD on early academic skills, +0.16 SD on executive functioning, relative to control. See also: Chef or the Ingredients, Representativeness, False Positives, Spillovers, and Cost Traps each get the full study-and-citation treatment on the Principles page.
    A pilot staffed with your best people proves your best people work. It does not prove the idea does.

    Ask who staffed their last pilot with the A-team and most hands go up, on purpose, to give the idea its best shot. CHECC's design pushes against that instinct, and that story landed harder than Uber's because it argues with habit rather than just showing a neat result. Once you sit with List's point it is plain: stacking a pilot with your strongest people answers a different question than the one you thought you asked, and most teams never notice the swap.

    The mechanisms behind this session: False Positives, Representativeness, Chef or the Ingredients, Spillovers, and Cost Traps. Those are List's five “vital signs,” each with full study-and-citation treatment on the Principles page.

    Where Behavioural Economics Actually Sits

    Why does behavioural economics sit between the spreadsheet and the mystery instead of choosing one?

    Product, Design & Digital teams · the talk I keep opening with this chart

    I open every version of this talk the same way, because most rooms arrive with one of two wrong pictures. Either behavioural economics is “common sense with a fancy name,” or it is “psychology with extra steps.” Both miss the claim. If I do not fix that in the first five minutes, every later example lands as a trick instead of a discipline. So the chart goes up first.

    Economics studies incentives and decisions, which is another way of saying behaviour. Classical economics sits at one end: simple and scalable, because it treats people like spreadsheets, rational, consistent, always maximising. Psychology sits at the other: high on explainability, because it studies the real mechanism, but a full account of one person's decision will not scale into a rule you can ship. Behavioural economics sits between them on purpose, informed by psychology, still asking what to predict and when. Part spreadsheet, part Instagram: mostly rational, with specific, predictable biases on top.

    The concrete example: classical economics says a free product and a one-cent product should behave about the same, because a cent is nearly nothing. They do not. Zero Price Effect research finds that genuine free does more than lower cost. It changes the category of the decision. Demand for a free item jumps far more than a one-cent gap predicts. That is the line under the chart: classical economics misses it, behavioural economics catches it, and the reason is a specific, citable, replicated finding.

    *Plus sociology and anthropology. The chart uses two axes for clarity; the discipline draws from more than psychology alone.

    Three ways to describe the same line

    Three points on one axis, not three rival theories. Each one trades explainability for scale differently.

    Classical economics

    Optimises for a rational actor

    Stable preferences, full information, no shortcuts. Cheap to model and easy to scale, and confidently wrong about free products.

    Psychology

    Explains the actual mechanism

    Closest to what is really happening in someone's head. A full psychological account of one decision still will not scale into a rule for the next thousand customers.

    Behavioural economics

    Predictable irrationality

    Keeps classical economics' aim to predict at scale, and swaps “rational” for “boundedly rational, in specific and repeatable ways,” the trade shown on the chart above.

    The Principles page covers 52 of these specific, repeatable patterns, each with the study behind it.

    Why any of this matters for product and design work

    Once the definition lands, the room asks why it matters here. Product and experience work already has a one-line job most teams never say out loud: influence human behaviour, on purpose, toward an outcome. Jeff Patton's chain puts it plainly: find it → try it → use it → keep using it → say good things. That sits under the acquisition, activation, retention, referral, and revenue funnel a growth team already reports every week. If product work is behaviour change, a real model of how people decide is not a nice-to-have on top of the roadmap. It is what the roadmap is for.

    This session stops at why the discipline matters here. Mapping your own initiative's outcome chain, and where it breaks, is its own field session. Once you have a chain worth testing, Experiments is where the testing lives.
    “So it's just marketing with a science degree?”

    Fair pushback, and I hear it most times I give this talk. The honest answer is falsifiability. Marketing wants something to work and will keep the story if it does. Behavioural economics wants to know when a mechanism stops working, and for whom. That is why every principle on this site carries a named study, a sample, and a methodology critique, not only a claim. I used to leave that distinction for Q&A. Now it sits right after the chart, because the room trusts what follows once they have heard it.

    The Metric That Lied To The Whole Room

    Could the metric that convinced you it worked have risen without your change?

    Product, Design & Digital teams · a talk built around a real Airbnb reveal

    In almost every room, usually from the most senior person, someone asks: “why can't we just make the change and monitor a metric?” Fair question. A controlled test costs time, traffic, and engineering a simple launch does not. Instead of arguing, I show a real chart and let the room talk itself into the wrong answer first. It is a live demo, not my own controlled study, and it is the fastest way I have found to make the risk felt rather than merely stated.

    A live demo, not a controlled study
    Room sees the real search-page redesign: a plain min/max price slider (control) vs. $/$$/$$$/$$$$ tier buttons (treatment)
    The metric climbs right through the window the new filter shipped in. I ask: why do you think engagement went up?
    The room always has good theories: the buttons are faster to scan, price tiers match how people actually search, the slider was fiddly on mobile. Every one sounds right.
    I lied to you: it didn't work, it performed worse. The overall trend never indicated that

    Hindsight bias plus a good story can sell a false belief, and that is dangerous because it feels like evidence. Every theory the room offered was plausible, consistent, and fitted to the chart. In Airbnb's own case the controlled result (the new filter underperformed and was not launched) stayed invisible. The chart did not lie. It never held the answer in the first place.

    Why it landed: Nobody gets to feel smug. I ask for theories before any reveal, and every theory fails the same way. See the full write-up: Hindsight Bias.

    Why $350,000 suddenly looks cheap

    How does putting $6,495,000 beside $350,000 turn the smaller number into a bargain?

    Agile Australia 2023 · Hilton Sydney · a talk on product, measurement & behavioural economics, to 200+ product and agile leaders

    Agile Australia put 200+ product and agile leaders in front of me for about 45 minutes. I had given a version of this talk before. This was the first time I built the whole thing around one frame: a “10x” way of judging product work, and why most organisations never get there.     From the actual talk: AgileAus23, August 2023.

    Live reveal, not a controlled study
    A room of 200+ people, shown one number: $350,000. “Is this cheap?”
    Right next to it: $6,495,000, a yacht, at the same international boat show a $350,000 luxury car was also on display at.
    The room's read on “cheap” flipped, on the same car, same price

    It fails a rationalistic check, and that is the point. $350,000 did not change. The number beside it did. Luxury car makers do not pick international boat shows by accident. Surrounded by prices with an extra digit, the setting is a chosen anchor as much as a matching audience.

    Why it landed: Same demo, adult-sized numbers. The mechanism matches a $160 price struck through to $80, with three more zeros. Full write-up: Anchoring.

    The second reveal used a different lever: free. Ask how many people buy coffee on a workday and the guess lands near 50%. Ask about weekly ice cream and it drops near zero, until the ice cream is free and suddenly everyone is in the queue. Same chocolate-pricing result I cite on this site's Zero Price Effect entry. Drop a luxury option from 15¢ to free and a cheaper option from 1¢ to free, and demand for the free luxury jumps far more than the 1-cent gap predicts. Two reveals, two audience guesses, both wrong in the same direction, because neither number was ever judged on its own.

    Three common mistakes

    The two reveals were the hook. This next section is for people who already know the terms, because that is where these three mistakes usually start.

    Reading the wrong layer

    The headline, not the literature

    A book chapter or LinkedIn summary of a study is someone else's compressed take on methodology, effect size, and caveats. Skip straight to the finding and you inherit their compression errors as if they were your facts.

    Overreach

    Biases thrown around like candy

    Every workshop now has someone naming a bias to explain a result nobody has tested. A label is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis, and it still owes you a measurement before it explains anything.

    Good intentions, no measurement

    Assuming it worked

    A well-intentioned change shipped without a control group will always look like it worked, because nothing is there to disagree with the story. Confidence of impact lives on the far side of a measurement, not a launch.

    Why it landed: All three are the same failure shape: swapping something easier (a headline, a label, a good intention) for the harder thing it stands in for (the study, the test, the measurement).

    Quick fire, real examples

    The back half moved fast: roughly one room-tested example a minute, no slide longer than the reveal needed.

    Attraction Effect

    20 nuggets for $13.70, or 24 for $11.95?

    More nuggets for less money makes the bigger box look like the only sane pick. The same “irrelevant” option does the deciding in a pricing menu as it does in a chicken shop.

    Default Effect

    The setting nobody switches off

    An opt-out pre-selected in your favour costs the other side nothing to leave alone and everything to notice. That is hidden money for whoever owns the default.

    Salience

    “Try free for 30 days!”

    The trial sits in size 40 font; the price that starts on day 31 sits in size 10. Both are true. Only one gets looked at.

    One I flagged as shaky, on purpose: a well-known finding that signing a declaration at the top of a form (before filling it in) makes people more honest than signing at the bottom. Clever result, and it failed to replicate at scale in a 2020 registered replication, years after it shaped real tax and insurance forms. I close with it as a caution, not a case study: read the methodology, check who has replicated it, and stay skeptical of a result just because it is well cited.

    1 Name the behaviour you actually want to change. Not the feature, not the metric: the specific human decision the work is trying to move.
    2 Understand the behavioural barrier before reaching for a solution, go to the actual research, not a secondhand summary of it, for why people currently do the other thing.
    3 Be precise about the intervention and the theory behind it. “We're adding social proof” is not a theory; “we expect visible uptake numbers to reduce perceived risk for undecided users” is one you can actually be wrong about.
    4 Predict the change and how you'll know: write the expected direction and rough size down before launch, where you can't edit it once you've seen the number.
    5 Measure with a real control group, then interpret. A before/after on the same population answers a different, easier question than the one you meant to ask.

    This is “doing outcomes well” in practice. Skip step 1 or step 5 and everything in between is a well-produced guess.

    The mechanism behind this: Confidence of impact only exists on the far side of a real measurement. See The Metric That Lied To The Whole Room for what happens when that step gets skipped.
