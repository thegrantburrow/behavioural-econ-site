    Special Report

    The test was real. The conclusion wasn't.

    When a famous study or experiment collapses, fraud and sloppiness are the usual suspects. The failures that show up more often are duller. Sometimes an honest study never held enough data for the claim it made. Sometimes a genuine result from one sample, one site, or one afternoon got promoted into a law of nature. Product and marketing testing add a third pattern: a change built on intuition alone, with a metric that moved and no check that the metric reaches the outcome that matters. This field guide walks through thirteen real cases of those three failure modes, the habit that would have caught each one, and whether anyone ever ran the check.

    The story in five parts

    A p-value under 0.05 isn't proof of anything

    Under pure chance, a pattern that large would be rare. Size, importance, and truth sit outside that statement.

    Small samples can't reliably see what they're looking for

    With low power, real effects get missed. The “significant” hits that do clear the bar usually look bigger than they are.

    One place is not everywhere

    Twenty-four volunteers, or one 1920s factory, support a claim about that group. Humanity is a different claim.

    Guessing without a theory runs out of road fast

    Copy tactics with no mechanism and the try-list stays short. After that, there is nothing left to generate.

    A rising metric doesn't prove the real outcome moved

    Clicks, opens, and time-on-page can rise while the outcome they are meant to forecast sits still or drops.

    The evidence at a glance

    8 of 9 experiments “worked”

    In Bem's 2011 precognition papers, 8 of 9 experiments cleared significance. Three labs later ran a pre-registered replication and recovered none of it.

    2.4 million responses, still wrong

    Literary Digest's 1936 poll collected 2.4 million responses, then missed the winner by 19 points. Who mailed a ballot back did most of the skewing.

    A famous effect, cut in half

    A Marshmallow Test replication with ten times the original sample roughly halved the effect. Income controls left almost nothing.

    36 students funded a statewide policy

    Thirty-six college students and a brief spatial-task bump produced the Mozart Effect. Georgia still mailed every newborn a classical CD on that basis.

    Netflix's own model, checked against reality

    Against 200 live A/B tests, Netflix's surrogate model matched the true launch call only 65 to 79% of the time.

    Three terms, used constantly, understood loosely

    Fraud is not required for any case below. Misreading one of three ordinary statistical ideas explains the first ten, so those ideas need plain definitions before the examples start.

    Concept

    Statistical power

    If the effect is real, will a study this size even be able to see it?

    Statistical power is the chance a study detects a real effect, given its sample size and the effect's true size. Designs with 20 or 30 people per group, the scale of several cases below, can sit under a 50 percent chance of catching a real moderate effect even when that effect is there. Misses are one half of the problem. The other half is uglier: when a small study still reports something “significant,” the true effect is usually smaller than the published number. Researchers call that inflation the winner's curse.

    What it teaches: Small samples do more than risk a miss. A hit from an underpowered study is usually oversized on paper, which is why striking early findings so often shrink once a larger replication runs the same test.

    The mechanism
    Small sample (20–30 people)
    A real effect exists
    Not enough power to see it clearly
    If “significant” anyway, the size is inflated

    Underpowered studies miss real effects and overstate the ones they catch. The published size is usually bigger than the true one.

    Concept

    Statistical significance

    What does “p less than 0.05” actually rule out?

    A p-value answers one narrow question: if the true effect were zero, how often would random noise alone produce data this striking? Below 0.05 means less than a 5 percent chance under that single assumption. The number does not rank effect size, does not give the probability the claim is true, and does not forecast replication. Run several outcomes and publish only the one that clears the bar, or drop a few “messy” points, and noise can manufacture significance on demand. That habit now goes by p-hacking.

    What it teaches: “Statistically significant” sounds broader than it is. Under one assumption, a pattern is unlikely to be pure chance. Importance, magnitude, and whether the finding is real are separate questions.

    The mechanism
    Random noise, no real effect
    Try several splits or analyses
    One clears p < 0.05 by chance
    Published and cited as a real finding

    Enough tries, and noise eventually clears the bar. That's what pre-registration exists to prevent.

    Concept

    Confidence intervals

    A result can be “significant” and still be a coin flip's width from meaning nothing.

    A confidence interval reports a plausible range for the true effect, not a lone point. “Improved outcomes by 10 points (95% CI: 1 to 19)” can clear significance while spanning a barely-there effect and a large one; the study cannot separate those stories. Small samples widen the interval because the data cannot pin the true value down. Headline numbers still travel without the uncertainty attached.

    What it teaches: Two significant results can differ wildly in precision: one a tight estimate, the other barely distinct from finding nothing. The interval is the honest report; the headline number alone is not.

    The mechanism
    One headline number: “+10 points”
    Real range: 95% CI, 1 to 19
    Spans barely-there and genuinely large

    The range is the honest version of the result. A single point estimate hides how uncertain it really is.

    Part one: small samples, misread significance

    Power posing, ego depletion, facial feedback, and the original elderly-priming study already sit as case studies on this site's Reading the Research page. The five cases here are a separate set aimed at the same failure mode from other angles. Flexible analysis. A brittle model. A bias baked into the method. And a sample so large that nobody bothered with the one check that mattered.

    The pattern across all five
    Small sample or flexible analysis
    Noise looks like a real pattern
    Published, cited, believed for years
    Fails once someone finally re-checks

    What looks real and what is real are supposed to be closed by statistical power and honest analysis. That is the gap these five cases fell into.

    Significance

    The Nine Experiments That Found ESP

    How did a respected psychology journal publish nine experiments appearing to prove precognition?

    Cornell psychologist Daryl Bem published nine experiments in a top psychology journal in 2011. Eight reported statistically significant evidence that people could sense random future events before they occurred. Bem later said he had skipped pre-registration of hypotheses and methods. He had used the flexible exploratory analysis that was routine then: several ways of splitting and scoring the data, with whichever version cleared the threshold going into the paper.

    What it teaches: Leave the hypothesis, sample, and analysis unlocked until after the results are in, and a determined analyst can pull “significant” effects from almost any noisy dataset. Three labs later tried a pre-registered replication of one of the nine effects and found no evidence of it. The episode helped push psychology towards pre-registration as a default.

    Bem, D. J. (2011). “Feeling the Future: Experimental Evidence for Anomalous Retroactive Influences on Cognition and Affect.” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 100(3), 407–425. Replication: Ritchie, S. J., Wiseman, R., & French, C. C. (2012). “Failing the Future.” PLOS ONE, 7(3), e33423
    Significance

    Himmicanes

    Do people really underprepare for a storm just because it has a woman's name?

    A 2014 paper took 92 U.S. hurricanes from 1950 to 2012 and claimed storms with more feminine names killed more people. The proposed reason was that a “Hurricane Sandy” draws less seriousness than a “Hurricane Charlie.” Hundreds of news outlets ran with it within days. Statisticians who re-ran the work found the result leaned hard on a few unusually deadly storms and on particular model choices. Change the model, or drop a few outlier years, and the effect vanished or flipped.

    What it teaches: Ninety-two storms is a thin sample for this kind of modelling, and many defensible model builds exist. Researchers can land on the exact setup that yields a striking result without intending to. That flexibility is often called researcher degrees of freedom, close kin to p-hacking.

    Jung, K., Shavitt, S., Viswanathan, M., & Hilbe, J. M. (2014). “Female Hurricanes Are Deadlier Than Male Hurricanes.” PNAS, 111(24), 8782–8787. Critiques published in the same volume by Bakkensen & Larson, and Christensen & Christensen
    Significance

    The Hot Hand, Reversed

    What if the famous study that “debunked” a real effect had a bug in its own maths?

    A landmark 1985 paper treated the basketball “hot hand”, the idea that a player who just made several shots is likelier to make the next, as a myth. Hit-and-miss sequences looked like coin flips. That verdict held for about thirty years. In 2015, economists Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo spotted a bias in the original method. In a short finite sequence, the usual measure of “chance of a hit right after a streak” leans toward finding no streak effect even when the data are truly random. Fix the bias, re-run the original numbers, and real hot-hand evidence appears.

    What it teaches: Sample size and analysis flexibility leave other failure modes untouched. Sometimes the statistical procedure itself is biased toward a false negative. Null results draw less scrutiny than striking ones, so the bug can sit unnoticed for decades.

    Gilovich, T., Vallone, R., & Tversky, A. (1985). “The Hot Hand in Basketball.” Cognitive Psychology, 17(3), 295–314. Correction: Miller, J. B., & Sanjurjo, A. (2018). “Surprised by the Gambler's and Hot Hand Fallacies? A Truth in the Law of Small Numbers.” Econometrica, 86(6), 2019–2047
    Significance

    The Dunning-Kruger Effect's Maths Problem

    Does “the least skilled overrate themselves most” show up even in random numbers?

    The Dunning-Kruger effect, that the least skilled people overrate their own ability the most, is among psychology's most cited findings. The usual test sorts people into quartiles by actual score, then compares each quartile's average self-rating to its score. Statisticians later showed that exact procedure yields a Dunning-Kruger-shaped curve even when ability and self-rating are pure, unrelated random noise. Sorting on the same score used to measure error creates a spurious correlation and a regression-to-the-mean pattern by itself. Methods that avoid that artefact shrink the effect well below the original claim.

    What it teaches: A pattern that looks real can be mostly a property of how the analysis looks for it. Significance is not enough; ask whether the technique could carve that same shape out of noise.

    Kruger, J., & Dunning, D. (1999). “Unskilled and Unaware of It.” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 77(6), 1121–1134. Critique: Gignac, G. E., & Zajenkowski, M. (2020). “The Dunning-Kruger Effect Is (Mostly) a Statistical Artefact.” Intelligence, 80, 101449
    Confidence

    The Poll With 2.4 Million Responses, Still Wrong

    Why did a poll of 2.4 million people get a landslide election backwards?

    Before the 1936 U.S. presidential election, Literary Digest mailed 10 million straw-poll ballots and got 2.4 million back, an enormous haul by any standard. It predicted Alf Landon over Franklin Roosevelt, 57 to 43 percent. Roosevelt won 62 to 37. The mailing list, drawn from telephone numbers, car registrations, and club memberships, tilted toward wealthier households in the Depression. A modern reanalysis found an even larger driver: the roughly 76 percent who never returned a ballot leaned toward Roosevelt far more than the 24 percent who did.

    What it teaches: Millions of responses cannot rescue a sample that fails to represent the population it claims to describe. Survey statistics' oldest lesson is still the first check on any “huge dataset” claim: huge relative to what, and who is absent?

    Reanalysis: Lohr, S. L., & Brick, J. M. (2017). “Roosevelt Predicted to Win: Revisiting the 1936 Literary Digest Poll.” Statistics, Politics and Policy, 8(1), 65–84

    The four cases already on this site belong to the same family. Power posing, ego depletion, the facial feedback hypothesis, and the original elderly-priming study share one core problem: a sample too small, or a measurement too unblinded, for the claim stacked on top.

    Part two: one study, one place, extended everywhere

    These five are not underpowered in the same statistical sense. Several are carefully documented, honestly reported findings. What failed came afterwards: a result from one narrow sample, one factory, or one afternoon got stretched into a claim about people or policy far past what the original design could carry.

    The pattern across all five
    One narrow sample or setting
    A real, honest effect there
    Extended into a claim about everyone
    Breaks down outside the original context

    A result that holds inside one group still has to earn any wider claim. Each of these went wrong at the extension step, after the original finding.

    Overgeneralised

    The Marshmallow Test's Missing Context

    Does delaying a marshmallow at age four really predict your whole life?

    From the late 1960s, fewer than 90 children were offered one marshmallow now or two later if they waited. All attended a preschool on Stanford's campus, mostly from financially comfortable, highly educated families. Longer waiters showed modestly better later outcomes, and the study became a famous parable about willpower. In 2018, a larger, more economically and racially diverse sample of roughly 900 children showed the same basic pattern at about half the size. Once family income and early cognitive ability entered the model, the link nearly vanished.

    What it teaches: An effect can be genuine inside a narrow, unrepresentative group and still misstate the cause once a broader population is tested. “Delaying gratification builds a better life” overlapped heavily with “growing up with financial security lets a child feel safe enough to wait, and also predicts a better life for other reasons.”

    Mischel, W., & Ebbesen, E. B. (1970). “Attention in Delay of Gratification.” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 16(2), 329–337. Replication: Watts, T. W., Duncan, G. J., & Quan, H. (2018). “Revisiting the Marshmallow Test.” Psychological Science, 29(7), 1159–1177
    Overgeneralised

    The Prison Experiment That Ran Once

    Can 24 college students, in one basement, for six days, tell you what's inside human nature?

    In 1971, Philip Zimbardo randomly assigned 24 male college volunteers to play guards or prisoners in a mock prison under Stanford. The standard telling says guards grew cruel on their own, and the study became a textbook staple: situations, more than personalities, corrupt behaviour. There was no control group, no pre-registered hypothesis, and no real statistical analysis, only a dramatic narrative from one uncontrolled run. A 2018 investigation using newly available recordings and interviews found the research team had coached guards to be “tough,” which undercuts the claim that cruelty sprang from the situation alone.

    What it teaches: A vivid single case can feel like overwhelming evidence while missing the safeguards that separate a finding from a story: a comparison group, blinding, a real sample. It ran once, on 24 self-selected volunteers, with essentially no data analysis, and still shaped how millions of people were taught to think about human nature.

    Haney, C., Banks, C., & Zimbardo, P. (1973). “Study of Prisoners and Guards in a Simulated Prison.” Naval Research Reviews, 9, 1–17. Investigation: Le Texier, T. (2019). “Debunking the Stanford Prison Experiment.” American Psychologist, 74(7), 823–839
    Overgeneralised

    The Effect Named After Data Nobody Had Checked

    What happens when someone finally runs the numbers behind a term used in every management textbook?

    From 1924 to 1932, researchers at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works plant varied factory lighting and reported that productivity rose whichever way the lights moved, even when they dimmed. The claimed lesson was that workers respond to being watched more than to the change itself. “The Hawthorne effect” entered management language as the label for people changing behaviour simply because they know they are observed. For decades the original data were assumed lost. When economists Steven Levitt and John List located and formally analysed the records in 2011, the dramatic pattern largely evaporated. Day-of-week and pay-period effects explained the swings better than the lighting did.

    What it teaches: A field can embed a term in its vocabulary and teach it for generations before anyone analyses the data underneath. “Everyone knows this effect is real” and “the data show this effect” are different claims, and for the study the concept is named after, they pointed in different directions.

    Original studies documented in Roethlisberger, F. J., & Dickson, W. J. (1939). Management and the Worker. Harvard University Press. Reanalysis: Levitt, S. D., & List, J. A. (2011). “Was There Really a Hawthorne Effect at the Hawthorne Plant?” American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 3(1), 224–238
    Overgeneralised

    Ten Minutes of Mozart, One State Policy

    Does listening to Mozart make a baby smarter, or did the claim just outrun the study?

    In 1993, 36 college students briefly scored higher on one spatial-reasoning task after ten minutes of a Mozart piano sonata than after silence or relaxation instructions. The bump, roughly 8 to 9 IQ-equivalent points on that task, faded within 10 to 15 minutes. The original authors never claimed a link to general intelligence, children, or lasting development. Media coverage and popular books stretched it into “classical music makes babies smarter” anyway, and in 1998 Georgia's governor budgeted a classical CD for every newborn in the state. Later meta-analysis across many replications found any true effect is small and tied to certain spatial tasks, nothing like the popular intelligence claim.

    What it teaches: Claim size and scope can balloon between a study and the policy built on it without anyone along the path deliberately lying. A short-lived bump on one narrow adult task became a lasting boost to infant intelligence, and a government spent real money on the gap.

    Rauscher, F. H., Shaw, G. L., & Ky, K. N. (1993). “Music and Spatial Task Performance.” Nature, 365, 611. Meta-analysis: Pietschnig, J., Voracek, M., & Formann, A. K. (2010). “Mozart Effect-Shmozart Effect.” Intelligence, 38(3), 314–323
    Overgeneralised

    A Magazine Essay Became a National Policing Strategy

    How much data was behind the idea that one broken window invites more crime?

    James Q. Wilson and George Kelling's 1982 Atlantic essay argued that visible disorder, a broken window left unrepaired, signals that nobody is watching and invites more serious crime. The piece offered no original data or study; it was theory plus anecdote. It still became the intellectual basis for “zero-tolerance” and “order-maintenance” policing at city scale, most famously in 1990s New York, and sits among the most influential ideas in criminology. Decades later, a National Research Council review and later meta-analyses found little support for the claim that disorder itself causes serious crime. The most careful work found a modest crime drop from disorder policing, concentrated in cooperative, community-based approaches rather than the aggressive enforcement the essay's name came to stand for.

    What it teaches: An intuitive, confidently argued idea can become national-scale policy without data behind it. The distance between a compelling essay and a tested causal claim never closed before the theory was already reshaping how millions of people were policed.

    Wilson, J. Q., & Kelling, G. L. (1982). “Broken Windows.” The Atlantic. Review: Braga, A. A., Welsh, B. C., & Schnell, C. (2015). “Can Policing Disorder Reduce Crime?” Journal of Research in Crime and Delinquency, 52(4), 567–588

    Part three: testing without a theory of why

    The first ten cases are honest studies undone by a statistics problem. This last set is different, and it is the failure mode most product and marketing teams meet week to week: a test that never had a behavioural hypothesis underneath it. Intuition about what might work, a metric that moved, and no check that the metric predicts the outcome the business actually cares about.

    Strategy fails first here. Better statistics cannot repair a missing theory. Teams that test button colours, headline wording, or badge placement with no account of the psychological mechanism at work can assemble a short list of plausible tweaks, then stall. Nothing generates the next idea except whatever is left to try. A real behavioural mechanism of the kind cited across this site does the reverse. One theory (loss aversion, social proof, the goal-gradient effect) predicts a family of testable changes across a product, not only the screen someone happened to be staring at. That is what lets a team place a strategic bet instead of nudging one funnel stage forever.

    The second half makes the first worse. A solid, statistically clean lift in engagement, more clicks, more opens, more time on a page, is often treated as if it must cascade into the outcome that matters: revenue, retention, real behaviour change. That link is often never tested. Sometimes it holds. Sometimes the two move in opposite directions.

    The pattern behind both
    No behavioural mechanism, just a guess
    Tweak something, a metric moves
    Assume it cascades to the real outcome
    Never validated. Sometimes it doesn't.

    A moved metric is still only a moved metric. Without a theory tying it to the real outcome, the graph is dressing a guess.

    No theory

    Ranking by Clicks Rewarded the Wrong Posts

    What happens when a platform optimises for clicks instead of the value a click is supposed to represent?

    Facebook's News Feed ranking once weighted how often people clicked a link. By 2013 and 2014, publishers had adapted with headlines engineered only to trigger a click, “click-baiting,” without saying what the story contained. Click rates on that content were genuinely high. Facebook's own data showed other signals moving the other way. People who clicked a click-bait headline spent less time reading afterwards. They were also far less likely to like, comment, or share once they returned, exactly the behaviours the click was supposed to forecast.

    What it teaches: Facebook had to rebuild ranking around bounce-back rate and post-click engagement rather than the click alone. The click had stopped meaning what teams assumed it meant. A bigger sample or a cleaner p-value would not have fixed that. The needed move was admitting the optimised metric had never been validated against the outcome it stood in for.

    Facebook Newsroom (2014). “News Feed FYI: Click-baiting.”
    No theory

    Even Netflix Had to Build a Model to Trust a Short-Term Number

    How much statistical machinery does it take before a 14-day metric can stand in for a real outcome 63 days later?

    Waiting months for every test's true long-term effect is costly, so Netflix built a “surrogate index”: a model that combines several short-term signals into one number meant to forecast a longer-term outcome. They checked it against 1,098 test arms from 200 real A/B tests. Even that purpose-built, heavily validated model matched the true 63-day result about 95 percent of the time, and correctly flagged which tests were worth launching only 65 to 79 percent of the time.

    What it teaches: A dedicated model, checked against 200 live experiments, is what Netflix needed to reach 95 percent confidence that a short-term number forecasts a long-term one. Treating an engagement metric as an “obvious cascade” down the funnel, with no model and no validation, is a reckless default anywhere.

    Zhang, V., Zhao, M., Dimakopoulou, M., Le, A., & Kallus, N. (2024). “Evaluating the Surrogate Index as a Decision-Making Tool Using 200 A/B Tests at Netflix.” arXiv:2311.11922. Framework: Kohavi, R., Tang, D., & Xu, Y. (2020). Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments. Cambridge University Press
    Experiment Teardown
    News Feed ranking, 2013–2014
    Same posts, ranked by a different signal
    Ranked by raw clicks
    Vague, curiosity-gap headlines rank higher
    High click-through rate
    Fast bounce back, little liking or sharing afterwards
    Ranked by bounce rate + engagement
    Descriptive headlines rank higher
    Somewhat lower click-through rate
    More time reading, more likes and shares afterwards

    The metric that looked better was the one predicting less real value. Raw click-through rewarded exactly the headlines least likely to satisfy the person who clicked them.

    Six questions that would have caught most of these

    None of the thirteen cases needed fraud to fail. Put these six questions to a finding or a test result before treating it as settled, and nearly all of them would have been flagged years earlier.

    1. Was the sample large enough to detect the claimed effect?Twenty to forty participants per group rarely powers anything but a very large effect. Ask what the study needed, not only what it had.
    2. Was the analysis locked in before the results came in?If several outcomes, splits, or models were available and only the working one got reported, a significant p-value proves far less than it appears to.
    3. How wide is the confidence interval, not just whether it excludes zero?Significance plus a wide interval is still high uncertainty. Two “significant” findings can carry very different amounts of real information.
    4. Does the sample resemble the population the claim is being applied to?Ninety Stanford preschoolers, 24 self-selected volunteers, and one 1920s factory are each a real result about a specific group, not a licence to generalise to everyone.
    5. Is there a real behavioural hypothesis behind this test, or just a guess at what might work?A mechanism generates the next ten tests on its own. A guess that worked once generates nothing else to try.
    6. Has the metric being moved actually been shown to predict the outcome that matters?Clicks, opens, and time-on-page help only if someone has checked they cascade to revenue, retention, or the real goal. Otherwise a rising metric is a fact about the metric, not about the business.
