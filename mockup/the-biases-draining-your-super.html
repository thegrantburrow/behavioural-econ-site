    The Biases Draining Your Super Could Also Fill It

    Most Australians never pick a superannuation fund, never pick an investment option inside it, and barely look again after the first contributions land. The system is built to run on that inaction. Defaults are the operating rule, and behavioural economics has spent decades measuring what defaults do.

    Defaults, loss aversion, present bias, and choice overload already appear elsewhere on this site. In superannuation they land at a scale few other domains hit: a compulsory system holding trillions of dollars, running for forty years, on decisions almost nobody actively makes. Aimed against a saver, those mechanisms compound for decades. Aimed for one, the same compounding works the other direction.

    Four documented cases show how those tendencies have cost Australian retirement savers real money. One 2021 policy change then used the same default lever to stop the damage.

    The story in four parts

    Inaction hands the steering wheel to the default

    From the first pay cheque, the fund and investment option that applied by default are usually the ones that compound for the next forty years.

    Selling turns a paper dip into a locked-in loss

    During the 2020 crash, loss aversion moved real members into cash at the trough. Everyone who stayed invested recovered the paper loss; the sellers did not.

    Today's need crowds out the retiree decades away

    An early withdrawal solves a present problem. The compounding that withdrawal would have earned over forty years never shows up on the screen at the time.

    Point the same default the other way

    A 2021 statute flipped the automatic outcome on job change from “open another account” to “keep the existing one,” using the identical default power that had multiplied accounts.

    The evidence at a glance

    10 million accounts nobody meant to open

    The Productivity Commission found roughly a third of Australian super accounts were unintended duplicates, draining members of $2.6 billion a year in needless fees and insurance.

    76,042 people sold at the bottom

    Across one 21-day stretch in 2020, tens of thousands of members at a single fund moved to cash while $34.2 billion was stripped from its value, freezing losses the market later recovered.

    $37.8 billion pulled out early

    3.05 million Australians took retirement savings out early in the pandemic. Counted with the compounding those dollars would have earned, a modest early withdrawal costs far more than its face value.

    One law change, one default flipped

    From November 2021, an existing super account travels with a worker to a new job by default. Opening another unintended account stopped being the automatic path.

    Skip the nomination, and the default writes the rest of the story

    When a new employee leaves the fund blank, the law routes contributions into a MySuper product: a standardised default built for people who never choose. That design rule is why most super accounts run on autopilot from the first pay cheque; a literacy shortfall does not explain a pattern this consistent. The fund and investment option that applied by default are the ones that compound, or lag, for the next forty years.

    Historically, each job start without a nomination opened another default account on top of whatever still sat from the last employer. The Productivity Commission's 2018 inquiry measured how far that pattern had gone. About a third of all Australian super accounts, roughly 10 million, were unintended duplicates. Each carried its own fees and, often, its own default insurance policy in the background.

    Real cost

    What a duplicate default account actually costs

    How does simply not choosing twice end up costing a saver tens of thousands of dollars?

    The Commission priced the leak at $2.6 billion a year: $1.9 billion in unnecessary insurance premiums and $690 million in excess administration fees. Some members holding several forgotten accounts, each with its own default life or disability cover, lost more than $50,000 from duplicate or unsuitable policies alone by the time balances were counted up.

    What it teaches: almost nobody opens a second super account on purpose. The default opens one whenever a saver fails to name an existing fund, and the fees and insurance premiums on that account keep charging whether anyone remembers it exists.

    Productivity Commission (2018). “Superannuation: Assessing Efficiency and Competitiveness.” Inquiry Report No. 91, Australian Government
    The mechanism behind it: this site's own Smart Defaults entry, and the full opt-in versus opt-out breakdown on Every Default Decides Who Pays for Doing Nothing, cover why a default this powerful was never a neutral, no-decision option to begin with.

    A balance that falls on paper stays invested until someone sells

    In a market downturn a falling balance is still a paper figure. It crystallises only when someone sells at the trough instead of waiting for the recovery that historically follows. Loss aversion makes that sale feel like prudence: watching a loss widen, even briefly, hurts more than the larger long-run cost of locking it in.

    March 2020 showed the pattern at scale as markets fell as much as 20% in a month. At AustralianSuper alone, 76,042 members switched their investment option in a 21-day window while the fund's own value fell by $34.2 billion. Across the system, the share of superannuation assets held in cash rose that month by more than the whole Global Financial Crisis produced.

    Real cost

    Selling at the bottom, then missing the recovery

    Why does switching to cash feel safer, right at the exact moment it does the most damage?

    Members who moved to cash near the March 2020 low froze whatever loss had already appeared, then sat out the rebound that arrived within months. One industry estimate put a $100,000 balance switched to cash at the trough roughly $50,000 worse off five years later than the same balance left alone.

    What it teaches: a balance that has fallen on paper is still fully in the market for the recovery. Converting it to cash at the low point is the step that makes the loss permanent, and that is the step loss aversion makes feel responsible.

    Robey, M., & Bruhn, A. (2025). “The Impact of COVID-19 and the ERS on Superannuation Savings in Australia.” Australian Journal of Management, 50(4), 1385–1413
    A note on sourcing: this site's network access blocked every attempt to reach the paper's own text, so the AustralianSuper switching figures above are drawn from contemporaneous financial reporting, not the paper's own numbers. The direction of the finding, real members converting a temporary downturn into a permanent loss, is corroborated across multiple independent reports from the same period.

    The same mechanism sits in this site's Loss Aversion and Risk Aversion entries, only here at a scale most other choices never reach. The Risk Aversion write-up puts it directly: a saver who avoids the market on a decades-long horizon “is applying a short-horizon risk judgment to a long-horizon decision.” A super balance is meant to be checked rarely and held for decades. A crash makes it feel like something that needs an immediate reply.

    An urgent present need outranks a retirement decades away

    A dollar needed this month feels far more pressing than the same dollar missing in forty years, even when the maths favours waiting. Present bias is the tendency for an immediate, concrete need to beat a distant, abstract one every time. Superannuation frames that trade-off cleanly: money is either available now or compounding for decades.

    The COVID-19 Early Release Scheme ran the trade-off at national scale. Between April and December 2020, the Australian Taxation Office approved 4.55 million applications from 3.05 million people, releasing $37.8 billion of superannuation early, most of it after a sudden loss of income. The scheme did what it was built to do for people in real hardship. It also showed, at a scale no lab study can match, how heavily an urgent present need outweighs a retirement outcome too far off to feel real yet.

    Real cost

    What an early withdrawal actually costs at retirement

    Why does a withdrawal that solves this month's problem cost so much more than its own dollar value?

    Every dollar taken out in someone's 20s, 30s, or 40s stops compounding from that day until retirement. What feels like a modest one-off adjustment today is that amount plus every year of investment growth it would have earned, gone from the final balance. The gap is easy to underweight in the moment because the cost is real, decades away, and easy to imagine fixing later.

    What it teaches: real hardship and present-biased choices can sit in the same decision. The scheme met genuine need for millions of people. It still shows, at scale, how automatically a distant retirement balance loses to an urgent immediate one, the trade-off present bias predicts.

    Australian Taxation Office (2021). “COVID-19 Early Release of Super.” ATO Super Statistics
    The mechanism behind it: see this site's own Present Bias entry for the underlying study on how steeply a reward's value drops the moment it's delayed at all, even by a matter of weeks, let alone decades.

    Extra accounts multiply the decisions nobody makes

    Each duplicate from the default problem above brings its own fee, and usually its own investment menu and default insurance as well. Optimising super then means several decisions instead of one. Choice overload often ends in no choice at all, which is how duplicate fees and unsuitable insurance keep running for years without anyone noticing.

    When a choice does get made, the naive “1/n” heuristic shows up in retirement investing too. People split attention or money evenly across whatever options sit on the menu, without weighing what each option actually holds. This site's Diversification Heuristic entry already documents that pattern inside a defined-contribution retirement plan.

    The two studies behind it: Iyengar & Lepper's demotivation-by-choice finding and Benartzi & Thaler's naive-diversification study, both already fully cited on this site's Choice Overload and Diversification Heuristic entries, are the underlying research this section applies to a super fund's own investment menu rather than restating in full here.

    Aim the default, and the same lever becomes the repair

    Each mechanism above depends on what happens automatically when nobody actively decides. That is why one well-placed default can fix a problem a thousand disclosures never would. The multiple-accounts mess is the clearest proof.

    From 1 November 2021, the Treasury Laws Amendment (Your Future, Your Super) Act changed the path for a new employee who leaves the fund blank. Instead of opening a fresh default account at the new employer, an existing super account is now “stapled” to the worker and travels from job to job. The default that had been creating a third of Australia's super accounts by accident was redirected to stop creating them.

    The fix

    Stapling: the default that stopped multiplying accounts

    If defaults caused the problem, can the same lever undo it?

    Stapling did not ask anyone to choose better, read a disclosure more carefully, or suddenly engage with their super. It changed the automatic outcome when nobody decides, from “open a new account” to “keep the one you already have.” The inertia behind the Productivity Commission's $2.6-billion-a-year finding is the same inertia the fix uses. The aim is simply reversed.

    What it teaches: a default is always a design decision. The live question is which outcome it steers people toward.

    Treasury Laws Amendment (Your Future, Your Super) Act 2021 (Cth), effective 1 November 2021. Australian Public Service Commission, Circular 2021/06

    The same logic already shows up elsewhere in super on purpose. Most MySuper products now use a lifecycle investment strategy that shifts a member's default allocation toward lower-risk assets as retirement approaches. Nobody has to notice the birthday or flip the switch. Australia's compulsory Superannuation Guarantee rate has also risen on a legislated schedule, from 9.5% toward 12%, lifting the whole population's savings rate without an individual opt-in. It is a compulsory rise rather than an opt-out nudge, yet the underlying idea matches this site's Smart Defaults entry on Thaler and Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow programme. Schedule the increase for later, and it never has to feel like a loss today.

    Four checks before treating a super balance as settled

    None of the four patterns above require carelessness. They are what a system produces when almost nobody is prompted to look, which is why one deliberate half-hour check still pays for itself.

    1. Was the fund or investment option ever chosen on purpose, or did the default fill both in?Either path can be fine. The gap is not knowing which path applied.
    2. How many super accounts sit under your name right now?The ATO's online services can list every account in a few minutes, including ones left behind from an old job.
    3. During the last real market downturn, did the balance get checked, and did anything change?A balance that has fallen on paper is still invested in the recovery. Moving to cash is the action that turns a temporary loss into a permanent one.
    4. What insurance rides inside the super, and is it needed?Default life, total-and-permanent-disability, and income-protection cover come standard on most accounts. Holding the same cover on more than one account at once is the most common way a balance erodes.
