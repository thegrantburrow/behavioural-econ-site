    Three Rules Behind a Real Classroom's Savings Habit

    In a real Year 8 and 9 classroom, the savings lesson arrived as a demo: divide every pay into three parts, label each part, and keep the long-term third out of daily view. Three hand-labelled envelopes sat on the table beside a slide listing three rules, and willpower lectures never entered the room.

    Those habits track three separate strands of behavioural economics research. One study frames a mental account, one times a commitment to a future raise, and one tests what makes a savings reminder land, each backing a different rule from that afternoon.

    The report covers all three findings, then fills the gap a school session cannot: the specific screen and tap that carry each rule inside a working banking app.

    The story in four parts

    A third, a third, a third, taught live

    Year 8/9 students divided pocket money into thirds, gave each third a label, and set the lasting third aside.

    Three rules, three separate real studies

    Automatic splits, named pockets, and accounts kept off the home list each rest on a different tested finding.

    The exact taps in a real banking app

    CommBank Pay & Transfer, rename, and hide-from-list cover the flow, checked against the bank's own support pages.

    The step most people skip

    Without a reason to look again, the habit fades. Field work has already measured that piece too.

    The evidence at a glance

    46% became 88%

    Changing which mental account absorbed a $10 loss nearly doubled how many people still bought a replacement ticket.

    3.5% became 13.6%

    A pledge made today against a future raise, leaving today's pay untouched, nearly quadrupled average savings at one workplace.

    Named goals, roughly double the effect

    Reminders that named the client's actual savings goal worked about twice as well as reminders that left the goal out.

    A live demo, three simple rules

    The session sat inside an “Everyday Banking” talk for Year 8 and 9 students at a real high school. The full recap lives in this site's field session write-up. A pair of sneakers got marked down live to open the room, and the useful part came next: what happens once money lands.

    The habit itself stayed plain. Every pay splits three ways: spend, save toward something specific, save for later. Treat those thirds differently: spend from the first, watch the second climb toward a named goal, and park the third somewhere it is hard to tap. Three hand-labelled envelopes made the split physical before anyone explained the psychology.

    Take away the room and the envelopes and the same three habits still form one connected sequence: pay arrives, splits three ways, each third gets a name, and the lasting third leaves daily view.

    Why the split has to land with the pay

    The classroom's first rule is about sequence. The split happens the moment the money arrives, before any of it has been mentally spent. That timing is the mechanism behind one of the largest workplace savings nudges ever fielded.

    Save More Tomorrow asks someone to raise contributions later, funded only from a raise that has not arrived yet. Present bias has no current cut to fight, and loss aversion has no drop in take-home to punish, because pay still rises at every raise, only by less than it would have. Saving more never requires feeling a pay cut.

    Thaler, R. H., & Benartzi, S. (2004). “Save More Tomorrow™: Using Behavioral Economics to Increase Employee Saving.” Journal of Political Economy, 112(S1), 164–187
    A field intervention inside one mid-size manufacturer's payroll and 401(k) system, followed across four real pay raises.
    StrengthGenuine field work with real payroll deductions, tracked through four actual raises over roughly 40 months at one employer, rather than a lab task or stated-intention survey.
    WeaknessJoiners had already turned down an immediate contribution increase, so the sample may have been unusually open to a delayed, painless version of the same ask.
    Key findings
    ParaphrasedOf 162 employees who had just declined an immediate rise in their contribution rate, 78% agreed to the plan instead, with increases timed to their next four pay raises.
    ParaphrasedJoiners lifted their average savings rate from 3.5% to 13.6% of pay across four raises (roughly 40 months), and 78% of joiners were still enrolled by the fourth raise.
    A note on sourcing: the published paper sat behind this site's network restrictions, so both findings above are paraphrased from the abstract and secondary academic summaries rather than quoted verbatim.
    See also: Smart Defaults, the full principle write-up this study anchors, covering the psychology of timing a commitment against a future raise so three separate biases get routed around together.

    Why naming and hiding share one mechanism

    Splitting early settles when the decision gets made. The other two classroom rules, name it and hide it, answer a later question: what keeps the money from moving again once it already sits in an account. Both rest on the same finding about labelled money getting treated differently from an identical unlabelled sum.

    The original study never mentioned savings accounts. People imagined losing a $10 theatre ticket already bought, or losing $10 in cash on the way to the same show. The dollar amount matched either way; only which mental account absorbed the loss changed.

    Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1984). “Choices, Values, and Frames.” American Psychologist, 39(4), 341–350
    Two independent samples, surveyed with one-sentence changes to which mental account a $10 loss was charged against.
    StrengthA clean, minimal manipulation: both groups faced an identical $10 loss, and only the framing of which account it came from differed.
    WeaknessA stated-intention survey with no real money on the line, so it shows how people say they would act rather than what they would do with a real ticket in hand.
    Key findings
    ParaphrasedOnly 46% of respondents who imagined losing an already-purchased $10 ticket said they would still buy a replacement.
    Paraphrased88% of respondents who instead imagined losing $10 in cash, with no ticket bought yet, said they would still buy one, a 42-point swing from an identical dollar loss.
    See also: Mental Accounting, the full principle write-up, including the study's own methodology critique and how the same partitioning shows up as a deliberate savings tool elsewhere on this site.

    That study tested naming and framing directly. It never tested hiding a labelled account from view, so this report treats the third rule as a reasoned real-world extension of the same mechanism, short of a separately proven finding. A named account already resists casual spending because it is filed apart from everyday cash. An account also kept off the daily list has one fewer chance each day to get reconsidered. The classroom's own version of this idea is already under a tighter test: this site's named-pockets experiment lays out how to check whether naming alone, without hiding, holds a balance just as well.

    The step most people skip: checking back in

    Steps 1 through 4 above run once and then run themselves: a standing transfer does not need a second instruction. Step 5 works differently. Hiding an account creates no reason to look at it again, and a savings pocket nobody reviews is easy to stop funding.

    A field experiment tested exactly that problem across three banks in three countries, with clients who had already opened a commitment savings account and set a real goal.

    Karlan, D., McConnell, M., Mullainathan, S., & Zinman, J. (2016). “Getting to the Top of Mind: How Reminders Increase Saving.” Management Science, 62(12), 3393–3411
    Three field experiments through three real banks in Bolivia, Peru, and the Philippines, with clients who had already set a real savings goal.
    StrengthReal clients, real accounts, and real reminders sent through three separate banks in three separate countries, rather than a single lab setting or one institution's customer base.
    WeaknessEveryone studied had already opted into a commitment savings account with a stated goal, so the effect applies to people already partway into the habit rather than to getting someone to start saving.
    Key findings
    Paraphrased, not independently verified against the full textReminders raised the odds of reaching a client's savings goal by roughly 3 percentage points, and raised the total amount saved at the reminding bank by about 6%.
    Paraphrased, not independently verified against the full textReminders that named the client's specific savings goal were roughly twice as effective as reminders that left the goal unnamed.
    A note on sourcing: every publisher and repository hosting this paper was unreachable from this site's own research tools, so both findings above come from search-indexed summaries of the abstract rather than a direct read of the full text. Treat the exact figures as a close paraphrase.

    Taken together, the four findings above cover the same ground the classroom covered in one afternoon. A mental account changes how money gets treated, a delayed commitment routes around the biases that fight saving more, and a reminder that names the actual goal keeps the habit alive. Each rule needs the others. Splitting money without naming it is just shuffling balances. Naming it without a standing transfer depends on remembering every payday. Automating and naming it without ever checking back still lets the habit lapse.

    Three questions worth asking before copying this

    1. Does the split have to be a third each?No. The mechanism is deciding the split in advance, on payday. The exact ratio is secondary. Equal thirds were the classroom's teaching ratio because they are easy to explain, and the demo never compared any other split against them.
    2. What if a bank doesn't offer named sub-accounts?Three ordinary savings accounts, each renamed, do the same job. Naming changes the mental account. The product label a bank uses for a “pocket” or a “space” is optional packaging around that.
    3. Does a reminder that doesn't mention the goal still help at all?The cited study found a generic reminder still worked, at roughly half the strength of one naming the actual goal. A vague “check your savings” alert beats silence, and it is still a weaker version of the same lever.
