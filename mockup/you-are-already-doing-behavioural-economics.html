    Special Report

    You're already doing behavioural economics.

    Checkout buttons, pricing tables, and product lineups already make choice-architecture calls. Naming them is optional. Making them is not. Someone sets a reference price, or they don't. Someone inserts a middle tier, or they don't. Someone pre-selects a plan, or leaves the field blank. A pricing page always has a design, deliberate or accidental. Neutral isn't on the menu.

    So the real fork is whether you know which call you're making. The eight mechanisms below are everyday examples, not a catalogue of everything that exists. They show up on pricing pages and product screens constantly. Each already has a full write-up on this site with the study behind it.

    Any of them can help a customer decide better, or push them toward a worse deal. If you can't name which one is running, you don't get to claim you chose the outcome.

    The story in four parts

    It's already happening

    Reference prices, pre-selected options, limited-time labels: pricing pages already make these calls, on purpose or by habit.

    Eight mechanisms, already proven

    Anchoring, the Decoy Effect, the Compromise Effect, plus five more. Each has a cited study already unpacked on the Principles page.

    The same lever cuts both ways

    Each mechanism has a clean use and a manipulative one. Often the tell is whether the underlying deal still holds once the framing is stripped off.

    Knowing the name is the fix

    This isn't an argument against behavioural design. It's an argument for being able to say which lever you're pulling, and why.

    The first number you see decides what every later number feels like

    Anchoring is the simplest of the lot, and it sits under almost every price on almost every page. Whatever number arrives first becomes the yardstick later numbers get judged against, even when that first number was arbitrary.

    Show a laptop beside a struck-through higher price, or show the same laptop alone at the same cash price, and you still have one product. The comparison decides how expensive that price feels. Anyone who picks a “compare at” figure, a “was/now” label, or which plan sits first in a table is already running this, even if they've never used the word.

    A third option nobody's meant to pick still changes which of the other two wins

    Anchoring sets a yardstick for one number. The Decoy Effect reshapes a whole set. Drop in a third option that is clearly worse, and people shift toward the option that now looks stronger, even though that option itself never changed.

    Take a subscription page with a monthly plan, an annual plan priced near two months of the monthly fee, and a decoy that makes annual look sensible. Annual becomes the obvious pick without its own sticker price moving at all. Nobody needs to buy the decoy. Its job is to reframe the other two.

    The option in the middle wins for being in the middle

    A decoy is built to lose. The Compromise Effect shows up even when every option is fair. Stretch a two-option lineup into three, and people lean on the middle more often than the features alone would predict, because the middle is easy to justify out loud.

    Basic, Pro, Enterprise: Pro often sells harder than its feature list alone can explain, mostly because it sits between the cheap end and the expensive end.

    Whatever's pre-selected wins, whether or not it's the best fit

    Anchoring, decoys, and compromise all need someone comparing. The Default Effect works when they barely choose at all. A pre-selected option wins far more often than the same option does when people have to pick it themselves. Even when the click would take seconds.

    Pre-select annual billing at checkout, or leave a marketing opt-in already checked on signup, and you're counting on the same habit: most people leave the existing selection alone.

    A limited quantity changes how much something is wanted, not how good it actually is

    Defaults ride on inertia. Scarcity rides on running out. Mark something as limited in stock or time, and demand rises right now, even when the product itself hasn't changed. Real shortage and fake shortage both pull on that wire.

    “Only 2 left in stock” and a checkout countdown timer lean on the same urge. Neither line has to be tied to a real, shrinking inventory to do that work.

    Other people's visible choices become evidence of the right one

    Scarcity borrows urgency from supply. Social Proof borrows confidence from other people. When someone isn't sure, they often treat what others appear to be doing as a signal of quality.

    “500 people bought this in the last 24 hours” and a review count placed ahead of the star rating do the same job. They stand in for a judgment the shopper hasn't finished making.

    Crossing $0 changes the decision itself

    Social proof answers “is this good?” The Zero Price Effect answers a different question: what happens when a cost hits literal zero. Free isn't just a cheap price. It's another kind of decision, and demand jumps hard the moment the number is exactly $0.

    “Free shipping over $50” usually beats an equal cash discount, because free sits off the ordinary price scale everyone else is competing on.

    The same saving counted once, on a bundle, outweighs the identical saving split across items

    Zero price cares about crossing $0. Bundling cares about how a saving is counted across items. The same dollar off feels larger when it sits on one combined bundle price than when it is split into separate item discounts.

    “Save $10 when you bundle” and “$5 off Product A, $5 off Product B” can be the same money, same products, and still feel different.

    Across these eight, the pattern is blunt. Reference points, option sets, defaults, urgency cues, social cues, zero thresholds, and how savings are framed are never idle on a pricing page. Something is always doing one of those jobs. The open question is whether anyone meant to choose it.

    Four questions worth asking about any pricing or product decision

    1. Could you name the mechanism, or only that “it works”?If the call was copied from a competitor rather than understood, you won't know whether it still fits once the product or the price changes.
    2. Would the underlying deal survive if the framing were removed?A “was $150, now $99” label and a plain $99 price should be selling the same product at the same price. If the framing is carrying work the price can't justify on its own, that's the tell.
    3. Does the customer end up better off, or only the business?An honest use still leaves the customer with a genuinely better decision. If it only moves the metric the business cares about, it's a different move wearing the same label.
    4. If a customer saw how the page was built, would they feel informed or played?All eight can pass that test. None of them need deception to work. The facts still have to hold once the framing is explained.
